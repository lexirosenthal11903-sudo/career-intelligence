# Career Intelligence — Emotional Vision Brainstorm
_Status: Complete_

## Summary & Key Decisions
_Session complete — 2026-06-06_

### The emotional north star
**Meraki → Satori → Kavanah.**
You arrive with your whole self. Something clicks. You move forward with intention.

### The founding truth
This product is personal. The founder is the user. The problem — not knowing what you want, let alone how to get there — is lived experience, not desk research. That's the rarest kind of conviction and the product's clearest asset.

### Who the user is (emotional portrait)
Someone at a genuine crossroads. Before they've sent a single application, they're already exhausted — by direction confusion, credential anxiety, parental pressure, the fear that their degree was wrong, the question of whether it's too late to pivot. They've been scrolling job boards for weeks and feel more lost than when they started. They're not looking for more listings. They're looking for someone who gets it.

### What the product should do to someone — the emotional arc

| Moment | Emotional target |
|---|---|
| Homepage — first 5 seconds | Trusted relief. "How have I not seen this before." |
| Input page | The moment of trust. Not a form — a conversation. |
| Loading screen | Seen, not processed. Someone thinking about you. |
| First results — direction | Satori. The path becomes visible. "I see you." |
| Daily check-in | Pride. Excitement to report back. Momentum. |
| Hard days / silence | Never guilt. You're back — that's what matters. Here's one thing. |
| Coming back after absence | Continuity. No gap mentioned. "Welcome back." |
| 3 months in | Someone who knows your journey and speaks directly to where you are. |

### The product's voice — permanent principles
- Always "I" and "you." A relationship between two people.
- Warm AND economical. Short sentences. Never a wall of text.
- Reflects back what the user said — proves it listened. Acknowledges specific details: what they've done, their direction, anything they included.
- Direct without being clinical. Honest without being harsh.
- **Reframes modern reality where relevant.** "In a traditional world you'd have needed X. Now you can come from any background — you just need to close the gaps." The product is current. It challenges outdated gatekeeping. It makes the user's real answer feel less risky.
- Never tells. Shows, asks, waits for a response.
- Note on Lexi's voice example: she gave the emotional register and structure, not finished copy. The actual writing needs a proper `/copywriting` session once design is locked. The blueprint is correct; the polish comes later.
- Test: *could a trusted mentor who just read your CV say this out loud?*

### The self-knowledge layer — key principles
- Privacy of a diary + attentiveness of a conversation.
- Cannot be the entry point — must be earned after the product has already shown value.
- Questions surface gradually over time, not all at once.
- Every partial answer is valuable. No completion pressure.
- The more open the user is, the better the output — honesty is rewarded, never surveilled.

### What the product must never feel like
- Clinical. Corporate. Like an AI.
- A job board or a dashboard to manage.
- Like it's disappointed in you.
- Like you owe it something.
- Gamified. Badges and streaks are the wrong register.
- Overwhelming — one thing at a time, always.

### What the product must never say or promise
- "We'll get you a job" / "Guaranteed" — never, until data proves it
- Statistics it doesn't have — only claim what is verified and true
- "AI-powered" — intelligence is under the hood, never surfaced
- Generic advice — the product promises specificity; generic breaks the core promise
- Flattery — "You're amazing!" — the voice is honest and direct, never cheerleading
- Urgency language that exploits anxiety — no countdown timers, no "act now"
- Anything that reads like it could have been written by ChatGPT
- "We understand how you feel" as a boilerplate opener — the product proves it understands by being specific, not by saying it

### The company vision
Stay independent. Become the modern equivalent of LinkedIn — not because of network lock-in, but because the product genuinely helps people and that reputation compounds into indispensability. LinkedIn became something everyone felt they *needed*. This product earns the same pull through actual value.

The ambition: essential, not just useful.

### The compounding intelligence principle
The platform gets smarter from every user. Every success, every outreach that landed, every role that was the right fit — all of it makes the next person's experience better. This is a founding principle, not a roadmap item.

### The design brief in single phrases
- Homepage: trusted relief
- Input page: the moment of trust
- Results: "I see you"
- Daily return: proud to report back
- Voice: warm, direct, economical, honest
- Overall: *"The job search should feel like a beginning."*

### Q6: What should the user feel when they open the product on a hard day — silence, nothing back from anyone?

**Lexi's answer:**
The user should feel **excited to open it**. Almost **proud** to tell it what they've accomplished. They want to update it on how their day has gone and see what's next.

This reframes the product's daily moment entirely. It's not a tool you check out of obligation. It's something you're eager to report back to — like telling a coach or a close friend your win. The product makes you want to share.

"Proud" is the operative word. The product gives you something to be proud of — even small progress. And it's waiting to hear about it.

**Key decisions surfaced:**
- The daily check-in isn't "here's what to do today" — it's "tell me what you did. I've been thinking about you." The user speaks first, the product responds.
- The emotional mechanic is **accomplishment + recognition**, not task management
- The product must remember. Walking in and having to re-explain your situation is the opposite of this. It already knows.
- The "what's next" reveal is a reward for showing up — not a to-do list, a next chapter
- **Pride as a daily motivator** — a user who feels proud of small progress keeps going. A user who feels behind stops.

**Also noted:** Company response time feature — a post-application journey map showing realistic timelines per company and what to expect (interviews, tests, assessment centres). Manages silence by replacing void with structure. Strong Phase 2–3 idea. See parking lot.

### Q5: Would a conversational format have kept you going?

**Lexi's answer:**
- Yes — conversation would have helped maintain momentum

**The single most important insight from this answer:**
The product's privacy is its superpower for self-knowledge. The user is alone with it. They can be more honest with it than with any person — mentor, parent, career coach — precisely because it holds their answers without judgment, without social exposure, without relationship consequence.

This is the design principle:
**The privacy of a diary. The attentiveness of a conversation.**

The self-knowledge layer must feel completely private and contained. Nothing shared. Nothing visible to anyone else. The product holds it. The user trusts it because it's not a person.

**What this means for design:**
- The questionnaire frame (even 5 questions in a modal) may carry the wrong emotional signal — "I'm being assessed"
- The questions need to surface gradually, at moments when the user is already inside the product and has already shown some trust
- Each question is optional and private — no sense of completion pressure
- The product responds to the answer in a way that shows it heard — not a survey response, a human-feeling acknowledgment
- The conversational energy (momentum, responsiveness) combined with the privacy of a diary is the target emotional experience

### Q3: What does the internal conflict actually look like — and what should the product do with it?

**Lexi's answer:**
The specific pressures (personal and real):
- Parent expectations pulling in a different direction
- "Did I do the wrong university course — has it set me apart from what I want?"
- "Have I been in the wrong field this whole time — is it too late to switch?"
- "Will this earn me enough money?"
- "Is the trajectory for this even good?"

What the product should do:
- Investigate how the person is feeling — find out IF they experience these things, and WHY
- **Listen first.** Not just process. Actually sit with it.
- If it's parental pressure: don't explicitly defy the parents. Find something relevant to the user that could *also* align with what the parents want — overlap, not confrontation
- If there's data to push back against the fear: present it. Show the plan. Make the real answer feel legitimate.

She's uncertain about the practical/ethical line here — how far should the product go into emotional territory?

**Key decisions surfaced:**
- The product's emotional mode is: *investigate, listen, then respond with data and a path* — not cheerleading, not therapy, not redirection
- The elegant move on parental conflict: find the overlap. Don't make the user choose between their parents and themselves — show them the version of what they want that satisfies both
- The product serves the USER's direction, not external pressure — but it does so by making the user's real answer *feel safe*, not by dismissing the pressure

**⚑ Open flag: Practical/ethical boundary of emotional engagement**
How deep into emotional territory should the product go? It's not a therapist. But it needs to hold space. The line needs to be drawn carefully — and the copy/UX for this moment needs a lot of thought.

### Email inbox integration (Phase 2 — HIGH VALUE)
Connect the product to the user's Gmail or Outlook via OAuth read-only access. The advisor automatically monitors for application-related emails — interview invites, rejections, offers, assessment centre bookings — and updates the application pipeline without the user having to manually log anything.

## Parking Lot Additions — Session 26 (2026-06-12)

### Auto-apply: Arlo applies on your behalf (Phase 5+)
Arlo identifies a role that's a strong fit, drafts the application and cover letter using the user's CV, outreach context, and tone profile, and sends it on their behalf. The user reviews before sending (or sets a confidence threshold above which Arlo acts automatically). Same mechanism applies to cold outreach emails.

**Why this matters:** Applying to hundreds of roles and receiving silence is the most demoralising part of the job search. Removing the mechanical load — keeping only the human parts (interviews, conversations, decisions) — is the logical end state of the "daily companion" vision. This is what "Kavanah — moving forward with genuine intention" looks like in practice.

**What it needs:**
- Email integration (Gmail/Outlook OAuth, send permission — higher bar than read-only)
- Application drafting from analyse output (already partially built in outreachContext)
- User review/approval UX before anything is sent
- Legal review: auto-sending applications on someone's behalf has employment law implications
- Cover letter generation (natural follow-on from outreach drafting already in the pipeline)
- Phase prerequisite: outreach drafting must be live and validated first

*Requires: Phase 3 email integration, Phase 4 outreach drafting, GDPR/solicitor sign-off*
