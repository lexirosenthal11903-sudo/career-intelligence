# Application Effectiveness Research — what actually works, UK early-career, 2026

_Started 2026-06-25 with Lexi. Purpose: ground every Step 2 generation feature (CV tailoring, cover
letters, the "why am I not hearing back?" diagnosis, outreach, interview prep) in evidence, not AI
guessing. This document is the gate before those prompts are (re)written. The advice the advisor gives
must be backed by what real UK data shows — and where it can't be, that must be visible, not hidden._

**Scope:** UK, early-career / graduate, current (2026 where it exists, 2025 floor). Nine threads.

---

## ⚠️ Confidence + verification status — READ THIS FIRST

Source rule (locked with Lexi, non-negotiable): **primary UK sources only** count as evidence — CIPD,
ISE (Institute of Student Employers), High Fliers, Reed's own recruiter surveys, ONS/HESA, gov.uk,
regulators (e.g. SRA), and peer-reviewed academic work. Career-advice blogs, résumé-builder "statistics"
pages, and LinkedIn posts **do not count**.

**The single most important finding of this session** (surfaced to Lexi 2026-06-25): when you hold that
rule strictly, the **structural facts about the UK market verify cleanly — but most of the popular
"application tactics" do not.** The 7-second CV scan, the "30–50% of hires come from referrals", and the
cover-letter read-rate stats all trace to US studies, recruiter content-marketing, or LinkedIn posts.
There is no UK primary dataset behind them.

So this document is **two-tier throughout**, and the tiers must stay visible when we write the prompts:

- **🟢 EVIDENCE (UK primary)** — verified, current, correctly attributed. The advisor may carry this with
  authority ("the data shows…").
- **🟡 PRACTITIONER CONVENTION (not primary-verified)** — widely practised and probably sound, but with no
  UK primary source. The advisor may give the practical advice but **must never dress it as evidence** — no
  "studies show", no invented percentages. Phrase as craft ("recruiters screen fast and in volume, so…").
- **🔴 MYTH / MIS-SOURCED** — circulating as fact, but the provenance is weak or wrong. Do not use, in the
  product or to anyone.

Nothing here is fabricated: no invented studies, no made-up numbers, all source URLs real. Where a figure
comes from a search summary rather than the primary PDF, it's flagged "verify at source before external use."

## Thread 3 — "Why am I not hearing back?" (the anchor thread)

_Built first because its quantitative claims anchor every other thread. This is a core feature: the
advisor must be able to give a **specific probable cause**, not "keep trying."_

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — the volume reality (this reframes the whole feature)

- **UK employers receive an average of 140 applications per graduate vacancy** — **the highest recorded
  in the three decades** since ISE began collecting the data in 1991 (2024–25 cycle). Source: **ISE Student
  Recruitment Survey 2025** (155 employers, ~31,000 hires from **1.8 million applications**), corroborated
  independently by **High Fliers, The Graduate Market 2025/2026**. _(Headline figures verified at source on
  ISE's free "Top 10 Stats of 2025" page; the full survey is paywalled.)_
- **The trend (verified):** 38 applications/vacancy (2002/03) → 86 (2022/23) → **140 (2024 and 2025).**
  Roughly a **3.7× increase in two decades.**
- **Sector spread is huge (verified):** **retail, FMCG and tourism roles average 290 applications per
  vacancy** — double the all-sector average. Competition intensity is highly sector-dependent, which the
  advisor should reflect honestly (a user targeting retail/FMCG faces very different odds from one in a
  lower-competition niche).
- **Why it's risen so fast (ISE, 2025):** the surge is partly fuelled by AI tools and one-click apply
  (LinkedIn "Easy Apply"), which let candidates generate applications at scale. ISE's Joint CEO states
  this has **pushed the quality of applications down** — volume up, quality down.
- **The market is tightening at the same time (High Fliers Graduate Market 2026, verified at source):**
  graduate vacancies at top employers were cut **−6.4% (2023), −14.6% (2024), −5.1% (2025)**, with a further
  **−0.5% forecast for 2026** — a cumulative **−24.5% slump since 2022**, leaving vacancy levels the **lowest
  since 2012** (High Fliers). _(The "larger than the 2020 pandemic / 2008-09 recession" comparison sits in the
  paywalled report and could not be confirmed from public sources: verify at source before using it externally.)_
  More applicants chasing fewer roles.

**What this means for a user — honest and true:** at 140:1, a strong application getting no response is
**the statistical norm, not evidence that something is wrong with you.** Someone who has sent 10–20
applications and heard nothing is seeing exactly what the base rate predicts. The advisor's first job in
this feature is to **recalibrate a distorted expectation against the real number** — then move to causes.
This is genuinely useful, genuinely honest, and no competitor says it.

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — hiring technology & AI screening

- **CIPD Selection Methods factsheet:** **78% of organisations increased their use of recruitment
  technology** in the last 12 months; **31% now use AI / machine learning** in recruitment (up from 16% in
  2022). _(Verify exact wording at source before external quoting.)_
- The CIPD lists structured interviews, work-sample/skills tasks and assessment centres as the
  higher-validity selection methods; references and unstructured CV-reading are weaker predictors.

### 🔴 MYTH — "75% of CVs are rejected by an ATS before a human sees them"

Ubiquitous, and **US-sourced marketing with no UK primary substantiation.** Do not use. The honest UK
version: large corporates and graduate schemes do use applicant-tracking and increasingly AI, but there is
**no credible UK figure for an auto-reject rate**, and SMEs frequently don't screen this way at all (see
Thread 1). The advisor should not tell a user "an ATS binned you" as if it's established.

### 🟢 / 🟡 — timing (UK hiring cycles)

- 🟢 **Graduate schemes overwhelmingly open Sept–Nov and run on a rolling basis** (deadline is a ceiling,
  not a target — early applicants are assessed against emptier cohorts). Investment banking opens earliest
  (Aug, can close ~Oct/Nov). Source: **National Careers Service (gov.uk)** + High Fliers.
- 🟡 The brief's "Jan–Feb and Sept–Oct high; Dec/Aug dead" framing is **directionally right but
  oversimplified.** The accurate, more useful story is **rolling deadlines inside sector-specific windows.**
  The advisor should say "this *scheme* is rolling — week 1 vs week 6 materially changes your odds", not
  "apply in spring." (Non-scheme/SME hiring is year-round and far less seasonal.)

### 🔴 — "3 in 5 applications get no response at all"

Widely repeated; traces to a **graduate-jobs.com press release**, not a primary dataset. The *direction*
(most applications get no reply) is consistent with the 140:1 evidence — but cite the volume data, not this
stat.

### Failure-mode map for the feature (what the advisor should actually diagnose)

When a user says "I'm not hearing back," the probable cause is usually one of these — and the advice differs:
1. **Base-rate reality** (most common, least understood) → recalibrate against 140:1; volume of *targeted*
   applications matters more than people think, but so does fit. 🟢
2. **Level mismatch** — applying above (or far below) their actual level. Recruiters reject fast on
   stated-requirement gaps. 🟡 (mechanism is convention; the *seniority ceiling* logic already lives in our
   matching.)
3. **Timing** — applied late in a rolling scheme, or to a scheme that's already closed. 🟢
4. **Targeting/relevance** — generic mass applications (now easier than ever via AI/one-click) underperform
   targeted ones; this is the quality-down dynamic ISE names. 🟢 (cause) / 🟡 (the fix specifics)
5. **Screening tech** — real for large corporates, over-stated as a universal gatekeeper. 🟡

## Thread 1 — CVs (UK early-career)

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — what UK recruiters say they want (Reed recruiter survey, named primary in brief)

- Reed's survey of **300+ UK recruiters**: the **single most important factor is that the CV is presented
  in a logical order** (≈half of recruiters); **91% regard a two-to-three-page document as acceptable**;
  **82% say following up on an application reflects well.** _(Reed is a named primary source in the brief;
  it is a recruiter-opinion survey, not academic — treat as strong industry evidence, not lab data.)_

### 🟡 CONVENTION — length for a *graduate* specifically

Reed's "two pages fine" is a **general** finding. For graduates the practitioner consensus (TargetJobs,
Prospects, multiple university careers services) is **one page unless there's genuinely relevant content to
fill a second** — a graduate two-pager padded with filler reads worse than a tight single page. This is
convention, not primary data, but it's near-universal among UK *graduate* careers professionals, so it's
safe craft advice. **Encode as: one page for early-career, never pad.**

### 🔴 MYTH (mis-sourced) — "recruiters spend 7 seconds on your CV"

The "7-second scan" originates from a **2018 US Ladders eye-tracking study** (7.4s). Reed popularised a
"7-second test" framing, but **there is no UK primary measurement** and the number is contested even in the
industry. The *underlying truth* — recruiters screen **fast and in volume**, deciding in seconds whether to
read on — is sound and consistent with the 140:1 reality. **Encode the behaviour, drop the number:** "the
top third of the first page has to earn the rest of the read." Never "studies show 7 seconds."

### 🟡 CONVENTION — what makes bullets land; ATS formatting

- Strong bullets = **action + specific contribution + outcome/evidence**, concrete over generic. Convention,
  but it aligns with the strengths/competency evidence in Thread 8 (employers want demonstrated behaviour).
- ATS-safe formatting (standard headings, no text-in-images/tables for key data, common file types,
  job-title/keyword alignment to the advert) is **sound defensive practice for large-corporate / scheme
  applications**, and harmless elsewhere. But it is convention, and its importance is **route-dependent**:
  decisive for big-corporate ATS pipelines, near-irrelevant for an SME where a human opens the file.
  **Encode as conditional advice keyed to firm size/route, not a universal "beat the ATS" command.**

### Graduate vs experienced-hire CVs (structural — 🟡 with 🟢 backing)

A graduate CV is read by different people (campus/early-careers recruiters, junior HR, or — at small firms —
the hiring manager directly) and **on different criteria**: potential, motivation, transferable evidence
from study/projects/part-time work, not a track record. This dovetails with the strengths-based shift
(Thread 8, 🟢): for graduates, **prior experience is a weak predictor of performance**, so the CV's job is
to evidence *behaviours and motivation*, not pretend to seniority.

## Thread 2 — Cover letters (UK, 2026)

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — what we can actually stand on

- We have **no clean UK primary figure for "what % of UK employers read cover letters."** Every "83% read
  them / 50% don't" stat traces to **US résumé-builder blogs and LinkedIn posts** — 🔴 do not cite.
- What *is* 🟢 evidenced and directly relevant: ISE 2025 notes candidate AI use is **"largely restricted
  to drafting covering letters and CVs or completing online application questions"**, and **46% of employers
  have no problem with AI use** (only 10% ban/detect it). The cover letter is therefore the document **most
  saturated with AI generation** — which is exactly why an authentic one stands out, and why employers'
  concern is AI *misrepresenting* ability, not AI per se (Thread 9).

### 🟡 CONVENTION — what works (sound craft, label as craft)

- **Length: shorter than people think for the UK** — roughly half a page to one page, 3–4 tight
  paragraphs. Convention, but consistent across UK graduate careers services.
- **Openers that kill it:** "I am writing to apply for…", "Dear Sir/Madam" on a named-contact role, and a
  restatement of the CV. **What works:** a specific, genuine reason for *this* employer/role and evidence the
  applicant actually understands what the organisation does.
- **The honest UK answer to "do they get read?":** *it varies by route* — more likely read in
  direct/SME/values-led applications; often skimmed or skipped in high-volume schemes where structured
  application questions and tests do the filtering. The advisor should be honest that a cover letter is
  **higher-leverage for smaller/direct employers** than for a big scheme with its own online form.

## Thread 9 — AI detection & the authenticity problem (cross-cutting, 2026)

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — and it **overturns part of the brief's assumption**

The brief assumed "active AI detection is happening; authentic voice is needed to *evade detection*." The UK
primary data (ISE 2025, verified at source) tells a more useful — and almost opposite — story:

- Employers are **largely permissive, not policing:** **46% have no problem with candidates using AI** in
  the process; only **10% have banned it or introduced technical measures to detect/prevent it.** The frame
  is **not** "get caught by a detector and rejected" — detection is the minority response.
- AI use is **concentrated in exactly the documents we generate:** ISE notes candidate AI use is "largely
  restricted to drafting covering letters and CVs or completing online application questions." So our outputs
  sit in the most AI-saturated part of the whole process.
- The real damage is **quality collapse and signal loss:** AI + one-click apply drove volume to a
  three-decade-record 140:1 and, per ISE's Joint CEO, **pushed application quality down.** When everyone
  submits competent, generic, AI-shaped applications, **none of them carry signal.**
- Employers are responding **structurally, not with detectors:** **79% are redesigning or reviewing their
  recruitment because of candidate AI use** (about **one-third redesigning selection**, **46% reviewing**) —
  moving toward harder-to-fake signals (strengths, structured/values interviews, assessment centres,
  verified work). The concern they name is **AI *misrepresenting* a candidate's ability** (48% of employers
  worried about graduates' AI use doing this; a 52% variant for school/college leavers is cited but could not be
  verified at source, so treat as 🟡 pending confirmation); undisclosed AI use *in interviews* was reported by
  **61%** (the strongest single figure here). _Correction: an earlier draft said "cheating doubled 7% → 15%" — that
  was a misread. The actual ISE finding is that the share of employers who had **never** suspected cheating fell
  from 22% to 15%, i.e. more employers now encounter suspected AI misuse._

**The strategic reframe for our product (this is the important one):** the line employers draw is **not
"AI vs no AI" — it's "AI used to present real information" vs "AI used to misrepresent who you are."** Our
edge is therefore **not** "dodge a detector." It's twofold: (1) **authentic, specific, genuinely-personalised
applications are now scarce and high-signal** in a 140:1 AI-flooded pile; and (2) **we sit on the right side
of the line employers actually police** — output built from the user's *real* examples and words enhances
true information, it doesn't fabricate a persona. That's what cuts through *and* what the restructured
processes are increasingly built to reward.

### 🟡 CONVENTION — the "tells" of AI text to design against

Inflated abstract vocabulary, symmetrical three-part lists, no concrete specifics, the same five adjectives
("passionate", "dynamic", "meticulous"), em-dash-heavy cadence, and claims with no evidence behind them.
The fix is not stylistic camouflage — it's **substance**: real projects, real numbers, the user's own phrasing.

## Thread 4 — Outreach & the referral reality

### 🔴 / 🟡 — the 30–50% referral stat: **NOT verified from a UK primary source**

The brief flagged this for verification. The honest result: **the circulating figures ("40% of hires from
referrals, 7% of applicants") are generic US/LinkedIn claims with no UK primary dataset behind them.**
**Do not use a percentage in the product.** What *is* defensible:

- 🟢/🟡 CIPD lists **employee referrals among the most effective and cost-effective sourcing methods**, and
  word-of-mouth/personal networks as a common and effective channel (CIPD resourcing materials; Labour
  Market Outlook). The *qualitative* claim — **networks and referrals are a major, often dominant route into
  work** — is well-supported. The *specific percentage* is not. **Encode the direction, not a number.**

### 🟡 CONVENTION — cold vs warm, what gets a reply

- Warm intros vastly out-respond cold outreach — universally held, no clean UK number. Treat as craft.
- A cold message gets a reply when it's **specific, short, low-ask, and clearly researched** (references
  something real about the person/their work, asks for a small concrete thing — 15 minutes, one question —
  not "can you get me a job"). Generic templated outreach is ignored at scale, same dynamic as generic
  applications.

### 🅿️ PRODUCT DIRECTION (Lexi, 2026-06-25) — *build the relationship, don't just draft the message*

Because referrals/networks are the strongest qualitative route, the bigger opportunity is helping users
**create the relationships that lead to warm intros**, not just write cold DMs — identify who in their
(or their second-degree) network is worth approaching, and build a relationship over time. **This needs its
own design/strategy discussion** before building. Parked, tied to the existing networking-feature plan and
the GDPR/solicitor question on contact discovery. Not built in this session.

## Thread 5 — Recruiter vs hiring manager (write for two readers)

### 🟡 CONVENTION (structural reality, well-established; not a single primary stat)

A CV is often screened **first by a recruiter** (agency or in-house talent team) whose job is *filtering* —
does this person meet the stated requirements, are they a safe pass-through, any red flags — and only then
reaches the **hiring manager**, who assesses *can they actually do this, would I want them on my team, do
they fit.* The two read for different things.

- **Agency recruiter:** works to a brief and a commission; filters hard against stated requirements; quality
  of screening varies (specialist vs volume).
- **In-house recruiter:** knows the culture and the manager's unwritten wants; filters for fit as well as fit-
  on-paper.
- **Hiring manager:** capability + team fit + evidence they can do the actual work.

**Firm-size pattern (🟡):** the larger the employer, the more likely a recruiter/ATS first-filter and a
scheme structure; at **SMEs the CV frequently goes straight to the decision-maker** (often the owner/manager)
with no recruiter layer at all. (No clean UK threshold figure — treat as directional.)

**Encode as:** the application must clear **both gates** — explicit, scannable evidence against the stated
requirements (for the filter) **and** genuine signal of capability and fit (for the manager). Our CV-tailoring
should make requirement-matching obvious *without* hollowing out the human signal.

## Thread 7 — The portfolio question

### 🟡 CONVENTION (structural, well-established)

For **design, content/creative, front-end & software engineering, and parts of marketing**, a
**portfolio / body of work is primary and the CV is secondary** — at graduate level too, often *more* so,
because there's no track record and the work itself is the evidence. "Good" at graduate level = a small set
of **real, finished, well-presented pieces** with the candidate's specific contribution and reasoning made
clear (process, not just artefact), over volume.

**Encode as:** for users in these fields, the advisor should **deprioritise CV-wording advice and lead with
"what's in your portfolio and how is it presented"**, plus (for engineers) public/demonstrable work. For
non-portfolio fields, don't push a portfolio that won't be read.

## Thread 8 — Interview prep (researched now to avoid a second session)

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — the strengths-based shift is real and UK-specific

- **UK graduate employers have moved substantially toward strengths-based interviewing.** ISE reported
  ~**50% of graduate-recruiter members used strengths-based recruitment** in some form, with **~69% blending
  multiple approaches** (competency + values + strengths). Named adopters include **Unilever, EY, Barclays,
  Nestlé, Aviva, Standard Chartered, BAE Systems, Royal Mail.** The **Civil Service "Success Profiles"**
  (gov.uk) formalises Strengths alongside Behaviours/Experience/Ability/Technical.
- **Why it suits graduates (🟢 rationale):** strengths interviews assess what energises and motivates you and
  how you'd *do* the job, not a track record — and **prior experience is a weak predictor of later performance**
  (long-standing finding in the selection-validity literature; verify exact citation — Schmidt & Hunter — before
  external use). This is the evidence base for telling an under-experienced graduate the interview is
  **winnable on motivation and behaviour**, not just CV.

### 🟢 EVIDENCE — what employers say graduates are *missing* (shapes what to prep for)

ISE 2025 (verified at source): **54% of employers say graduates fall short on self-awareness** (up from 43%
in 2024) and **46% on resilience** (up from 37%). This is the biggest stated gap — and it's exactly what
strengths-based interviews probe. **Encode as:** prep should build genuine self-awareness (knowing your real
strengths, motivations, and honest gaps and being able to articulate them), not rehearsed answers — that's
both what the format rewards and what employers say is missing.

### 🟢 / 🟡 — competency (STAR) vs strengths

- **Competency-based (STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result)** still widely used — structured, asks for
  evidence of past behaviour. Preparable with real examples. 🟢 (CIPD: structured interviews are high-validity.)
- **Strengths-based** — faster, less rehearsable questions about what you enjoy/do naturally; **you can't
  fake energy**, so prep = self-knowledge and authentic reflection, not scripted answers. 🟡 (mechanism is
  convention; the prevalence is 🟢.)

### The honest line (🟡, but important)

Interview prep changes **framing, structure, and confidence** (knowing the format, having real examples
ready, understanding the employer) — it **does not manufacture experience you don't have.** For our cohort
the honest, useful prep is: **map real examples to likely questions, understand the format you'll face
(strengths vs competency vs assessment centre), and research what the employer actually does.** No tricks.

## What this changes — translating research → product (for the prompt rewrites)

_The prompt rewrites are a separate, later step (per the brief). This is the brief for them._

1. **`why_not_hearing_back` (to build):** lead with the **140:1 base rate** to recalibrate, *then* diagnose
   from the failure-mode map (level mismatch · timing/rolling deadlines · targeting/relevance · screening
   tech where route-relevant). **Never** assert "an ATS rejected you" as fact. Honest, specific, sourced.
2. **`tailor_cv`:** write for **both gates** (Thread 5) — make requirement-matching scannable *and* preserve
   human signal. **One page** for early-career. **Substance over AI-shaped polish** (Thread 9) — real
   examples, the user's words, no generic adjectives. ATS-formatting advice **conditional on route/firm size**,
   not universal. Drop any "7 seconds" / "75% ATS" claims.
3. **`write_cover_letter`:** **half-to-one page**, specific reason for *this* employer, no template openers,
   built from the user's real motivation and examples. Be honest that it's **higher-leverage for direct/SME**
   than for a big scheme. Authenticity as *signal*, not detector-evasion.
4. **Outreach (to build):** **no referral percentage in the product.** Direction only ("networks are a major
   route in"). Cold messages: specific, short, low-ask, researched. The relationship-building product idea is
   parked for a design discussion.
5. **Interview prep (to build):** detect/ask the **format** (strengths vs competency vs assessment centre),
   prep real examples to questions, research the employer; honest that prep reframes but can't fake experience.
6. **Cross-cutting (all generators):** authentic, specific, user-grounded output is the product's edge in a
   140:1, AI-saturated market — this is now **evidence-backed**, not a stylistic preference.
