## Bucket F — How skilled mentors handle abrupt topic-switching mid-session

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. GROW does not require a fixed agenda — Whitmore was explicit.**
Sir John Whitmore (_Coaching for Performance_, 1992/4th ed 2009) wrote that the GROW model should **not be
followed slavishly** — "a skilled coach moves fluidly between the four elements." The model names the _type_ of
question that is most useful at any moment; it does not mandate a sequence. Agenda rigidity that ignores what
the person is actually experiencing is explicitly a beginner error. **Framework-certain** (Whitmore is the
primary source; the anti-slavish-adherence note is in the text).
[Performance Consultants — the GROW model](https://www.performanceconsultants.com/resources/the-grow-model/)

**2. MI's "rolling with resistance" — the foundational non-confrontational move (Miller & Rollnick).**
In Motivational Interviewing, **resistance is not challenged — it is rolled with.** A person moving away from
a topic, changing subject, or expressing ambivalence is _not_ "wrong"; the practitioner's urge to correct this
is called the **"righting reflex"** — the impulse to point out the problem, correct the course, argue for
change. Miller & Rollnick identify the righting reflex as one of the main ways a practitioner accidentally
shuts the conversation down. The skilled move is to **notice the resistance and reflect it, not argue against
it.** Rolling with resistance means moving with the person's energy rather than against it.
**Framework-certain** — this is a core, named MI principle, primary source: Miller & Rollnick,
_Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change_, 3rd ed., 2012.
[MINT — understanding MI](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing) ·
[Relias — OARS and rolling with resistance](https://www.relias.com/blog/oars-motivational-interviewing)

**3. The "two-agenda" tension — held in coaching literature as a real structural problem.**
Coaching literature acknowledges the tension explicitly: a coach/mentor holds **their own sense of a productive
direction** alongside **the mentee's expressed need in the moment**. The agreed resolution in skilled practice
is: **the mentee's agenda takes precedence, but the mentor holds the thread.** "Holding the thread" means the
mentor internally tracks where they were and what was being worked on — not to police a script, but to be
able to name it gently when the time is right. This is described in facilitation and executive coaching
guides as a distinct skill: **tracking without controlling.** **Practitioner-only** — field consensus, no
single primary study.
[ICF — Core Competencies (Active Listening / Maintains Presence)](https://coachingfederation.org/credentials-and-standards/core-competencies)

**4. The "agenda check" as a standard session-management move.**
Both GROW-based coaching and the EMCC's competency framework describe an **agenda check** at the session
opening (and at natural breaks): "What do you most want to get out of today?" This moment also functions as
a **re-contracting point** if the user's immediate need has shifted since the last session. The implication:
topic switches mid-session can be handled by a brief, warm pause — "that's interesting — do you want to go
there, or do you want to stay with what we were working on?" — which gives the user agency without abandoning
the thread. **Practitioner-only** for the specific form; **verify-before-quoting** for the EMCC competency
attribution.
[EMCC — competency framework](https://www.emccglobal.org/quality/competences/)

### The concrete moves (texture)

The practitioner literature describes a short sequence when a mentee switches topic abruptly:

1. **Acknowledge the switch without judgment** — "I notice we've moved somewhere different." This names what
   happened without making it a mistake.
2. **Reflect, don't redirect immediately** — use a brief reflection of the new topic to show it was heard:
   "It sounds like [X] is on your mind." This prevents the person feeling ignored or overridden.
3. **Offer a choice, not a decision** — "Do you want to stay with [X] for a bit, or would it be useful to
   come back to [original thread]?" The mentor offers the map; the mentee chooses the route.
4. **Bookmark the original thread explicitly** — regardless of which direction is chosen: "I'm noting where
   we were — we can come back to that." This preserves psychological safety (nothing is lost) without forcing
   the person back.
5. **Follow if the person chooses to go there** — and trust that what surfaces may be more important than
   what was planned. The most significant insight is often what the person reaches for when they move away.

The error pattern to avoid: **immediately redirecting** ("let's stay focused on X") — this reads as
dismissive, activates defensiveness, and models that the person's instincts aren't trusted. In an anxious
user, it can shut down the conversation entirely.

### Translation — what this means for a text-only AI advisor + our cohort

- **In text, an abrupt topic change arrives as a new message on a different subject.** There is no "I notice
  we've moved" delivered with a warm pause and eye contact. The text equivalent: the advisor responds to what
  the person actually said (shows it was heard), then — in the same message — offers the thread back. "That's
  on your mind — [brief reflection]. Before we go there, do you want to pick up where we were on [X], or
  take this direction?" One move, not two turns.
- **Never ignore a topic change and continue as if it didn't happen.** This is the worst failure mode in
  text: the person says something new and the advisor returns to the prior question as if reading from a
  script. It reads as a bot.
- **The advisor holds the thread internally** — this means the system must track the prior topic in state
  and be able to surface it. "We were working on [X]" must come from memory, not a guess.
- **Offering a choice is the right move for an anxious user** — they feel neither policed nor abandoned.
  The choice itself is a small act of agency, which is exactly what anxious users often lack.
- **Psychological safety rule applies doubly here.** Edmondson: safety requires curiosity, not judgment.
  An anxious 24-year-old who switches topic mid-conversation should not be made to feel they broke a rule.
  The advisor's response to a switch is an opportunity to demonstrate that the whole person is welcome, not
  just the tidy thread.

## Bucket G — Distinguishing avoidance/flight from genuine need to switch

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. Procrastination as emotion regulation — Pychyl & Sirois (primary source, peer-reviewed).**
The most current, well-supported model of procrastination is Pychyl & Sirois (2016): procrastination is
**primarily an emotion-regulation strategy, not a time-management failure.** People put off or move away
from a task because the task generates a negative emotion (anxiety, fear of failure, self-doubt, boredom)
and switching away provides immediate relief. The task doesn't get easier — but the person feels better
*right now*. **Framework-certain** — this is the current scientific consensus; widely replicated.

Pychyl, T.A. & Sirois, F.M. (2016). Procrastination, emotion regulation, and well-being. In F.M. Sirois
& T.A. Pychyl (Eds.), _Procrastination, Health, and Well-Being_. Academic Press.
[Pychyl's procrastination research — Carleton University](https://carleton.ca/procrastination/) ·
[APA — Pychyl procrastination research overview](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/procrastination)

**2. Steel's Procrastination Equation — quantifying avoidance (peer-reviewed meta-analysis).**
Steel (2007) synthesised 691 correlations from 216 studies. His "Procrastination Equation": procrastination
increases when **task value is low, delay to reward is long, self-efficacy is low, or impulsiveness is high**.
Crucially for our context: **anxiety and fear of failure are reliable predictors of avoidance** — they
reduce perceived task value and self-efficacy simultaneously. An anxious user switching away from a task is
consistent with the avoidance model.
**Framework-certain** — peer-reviewed meta-analysis, top journal.
Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential
self-regulatory failure. _Psychological Bulletin_, 133(1), 65–94.
[PubMed — Steel 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201571/)

**3. MI's "sustain talk" vs "change talk" — the practical listening frame (Miller & Rollnick).**
In MI, a practitioner listens for two kinds of speech:
- **Change talk** — the person's own language moving toward action: "I want to…", "I need to…", "I could…",
  "I intend to…", "I've already tried…". Predicts actual behaviour change.
- **Sustain talk** — language that sustains the status quo / moves away from change: "But…", "I can't because…",
  "It's too hard right now", "Maybe later", "That doesn't matter", sudden topic changes.

A sudden topic switch mid-task is **sustain talk in action** — the person is moving away from a change
direction. This does NOT mean the mentor argues for change; it means the mentor notices the pattern and
can gently explore it. **Framework-certain** — core MI framework, primary source Miller & Rollnick 2012.

**4. The diagnostic question: urgent/external vs internal/vague.**
Coaching practitioners describe a practical distinction for telling avoidance from genuine priority:

**Signals of a genuine, legitimate need to switch (priority-driven):**
- The new topic is **specific and external** — a real deadline, a person they need to respond to, an event
  that has just changed
- The person names the reason clearly: "I just heard back about an interview — that's more urgent right now"
- The emotional tone is **grounded/purposeful**, not avoidant/scattered
- The switch feels **purposeful and bounded** — they know what they need from the new topic

**Signals of avoidance/flight:**
- The new topic is **vague or diffuse** — "I was also thinking about…", "something else has been on my mind"
- The person cannot clearly articulate _why_ it's more important right now
- The emotional tone is **anxious or scattered** — feels like flight, not focus
- The switch happens **at a point of challenge** — just when the prior topic required commitment, decision,
  or action (a classic avoidance trigger per Pychyl)
- The new topic would also require no concrete action — just more exploration, more thinking

**Practitioner-only** for the specific signal list (this is synthesised from coaching practitioner doctrine
and MI's sustain-talk framework, not a single study).

**5. The "righting reflex" — why the mentor must not immediately correct avoidance.**
Miller & Rollnick name the righting reflex as the mentor's own error pattern: the impulse to immediately
point out avoidance, correct the course, argue for the original task. **This impulse, acted on, reliably
makes things worse** — it triggers reactance (the person digs in), damages psychological safety, and
confirms the anxious person's fear that they are being judged. The correct move is to **notice and gently
name**, not to correct. **Framework-certain** — core MI principle.

### In text: how to read avoidance without body language

This is the hardest problem for a text-based advisor. Without tone, posture, eye contact or hesitation,
the signals available are:

**Linguistic signals (accessible in text):**
- **Timing of the switch** — does it arrive immediately after a question that required commitment or
  decision? (Avoidance signal — the task just became uncomfortable.)
- **Specificity of the new topic** — is it concrete and named ("the interview next Tuesday") or vague
  and general ("I've also been thinking about whether I should switch industries entirely")?
- **Whether a reason is given** — genuine priority shifts are typically explained; avoidance switches
  often just arrive.
- **The pattern across sessions** — does this person tend to switch topic when a specific kind of question
  comes up? A text system with memory can track this; a human mentor would note it in session.
- **The verb tense and agency** — "I need to do X" (change talk) vs "I don't know if I can" / "maybe later"
  (sustain talk). The language around the new topic matters too: are they moving toward something, or away?

**The honest limit:** in text, the advisor will sometimes not be able to tell. When the signal is
ambiguous, the right move is to **ask gently, not assume** — "What's making [new topic] feel urgent right
now?" This surfaces the reason without labelling the switch as avoidance.

### The concrete moves (texture)

**When the switch reads as avoidance:**
1. Acknowledge and hear the new topic briefly — do not ignore it.
2. Gently note the thread: "I notice we were just at the point of [X] when [new thing] came up."
3. Name it without judgment: "Sometimes things feel more urgent when we're about to do the hard part."
   (This is MI — reflecting a pattern without labelling it as a flaw.)
4. Offer the choice: "Do you want to stay with [new thing] or come back to [X]?"
5. If they choose the new thing: follow it — but hold the thread and return.

**When the switch reads as genuine priority:**
1. Follow without hesitation.
2. Briefly acknowledge the original thread: "I'm noting where we were — we'll come back to that."
3. Re-engage fully with the new topic.

**The error to avoid in either case:** labelling the person's switch as avoidance directly. "It sounds
like you're avoiding this" is a clinical observation, not a coaching move — it activates defensiveness and
shame. The mentor names the pattern, not the pathology.

### Translation — what this means for a text-only AI advisor + our cohort

- **The advisor cannot reliably diagnose avoidance from a single message.** It can notice signals. This
  is a probabilistic read, not a clinical assessment — and our product explicitly holds the "not a therapist"
  boundary. The move is to notice and gently explore, never to label.
- **The timing signal is usable in text.** If the advisor just asked a commitment question ("So what would
  you do first?") and the person's next message is on a completely different topic — that is worth noticing.
  The advisor can track this: "I see you moved somewhere else — that's fine. What made [X] come up just now?"
- **The specificity signal is also usable in text.** "I have an interview at 2pm on Thursday" is different
  from "I've been thinking about whether I'm even in the right field." The advisor can respond differently
  to each.
- **The anxious user needs the benefit of the doubt.** Steel's model: anxiety reduces self-efficacy and
  perceived task value — the avoidance is not laziness, it is a very human response to feeling overwhelmed.
  The advisor's posture toward any switch must be **curious, not suspicious**. Edmondson's psychological
  safety rule applies: the person must never feel caught out.
- **Pattern recognition across sessions is powerful here and is only available to an AI with memory.** A
  human mentor might notice over weeks; the advisor can notice within a session and across them. This is a
  genuine advisor-over-human advantage, used carefully.

- **CONFIRMS** psychological safety / curiosity-not-judgment as the foundational operating mode.
- **FILLS A CRITICAL GAP:** the persona has no framework for reading avoidance. This gives the advisor a

## Bucket H — Building follow-through for anxious users, without gamification

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999) — strong peer-reviewed evidence.**
Peter Gollwitzer's research (University of New York) established that **if-then planning significantly
improves goal completion** — "If [situation X] happens, I will do [behaviour Y]." This is not motivation; it
is a **pre-decided response plan** that offloads the decision from the moment of action. A large meta-analysis
(Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, _Advances in Experimental Social Psychology_) found a **medium-to-large effect
size** (d = 0.65) across 94 studies on goal achievement.

The mechanism: implementation intentions link the goal to a specific **trigger situation** (time, place, event)
so that behaviour becomes nearly automatic when that trigger appears — bypassing the emotional-regulation
failure that causes procrastination (Pychyl: avoidance of the uncomfortable feeling).

**Framework-certain** (peer-reviewed, replicated). A specific statistic: d = 0.65 across 94 studies —
**verify before quoting** (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006).
[Gollwitzer — NYU psychology](https://psych.nyu.edu/gollwitzer/) ·
[APA PsycNet — Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-12644-003)
_Cite as:_ Gollwitzer, P.M. & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement.
_Advances in Experimental Social Psychology_, 38, 69–119.

**2. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) — committed action, not motivation-first.**
ACT (Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999 — now a well-established evidence base) approaches follow-through
from a different angle: **defusion from the feelings that block action.** The core ACT move is to help
the person act *while* experiencing discomfort — not after it has resolved. This directly addresses the
anxious user: you don't need to feel ready; you take the action and the feeling comes after (or doesn't —
and that's okay). ACT uses **values clarification** (why does this matter to you?) as the anchor for
committed action, not external incentives.

ACT's approach to follow-through: **small, values-aligned actions taken in the presence of uncomfortable
feelings**, not despite them. "Committed action" in ACT is always tied to an identified value.
**Framework-certain** — ACT is an evidence-based psychological intervention. The mechanism's application
to coaching is practitioner-confirmed (Harris 2008, _The Happiness Trap_; coaching literature).
[ACBS — Association for Contextual Behavioral Science (ACT evidence base)](https://contextualscience.org/act) ·
[Psychology Today — ACT explained](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy)

**3. Matthews (2014, Dominican University) — written, shared goals.**
The study most commonly cited as the accountability anchor: goals written down AND shared with a supportive
other showed meaningfully higher completion rates than goals merely thought about. The mechanism: writing
externalises the commitment; sharing it creates social accountability.
**Verify-before-quoting** — small, unpublished-in-a-top-journal study; the effect direction is credible
but the precision of any percentage claim is not. The parent doc (mentorship-research.md) already flags
this study's limits. Do not over-claim.
[Dominican University — Matthews 2014 study](https://www.dominican.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/gailmatthews-harvard-goals-researchsummary.pdf)

**4. MI commitment language — "change talk" predicts action (Miller & Rollnick).**
In MI, the research finding is that **the strength and specificity of a person's own commitment language
is the best predictor of whether they follow through.** The more specific and self-generated the commitment
("I will send the email by Thursday evening"), the more likely the action. The practitioner's job is to
draw out and **reflect** the commitment, not supply it. A therapist/advisor who imposes a commitment
("you should do X by Friday") bypasses the mechanism — it works from outside motivation, which is less
durable. **Framework-certain** — core MI finding. The research base for this is strongest in clinical
health behaviour change; the transfer to coaching is well-accepted but less studied.

**5. Framing return to unfinished things — the safe way, for an anxious person.**
The coaching literature on accountability (CoachVantage, Bailey Balfour in the parent doc) is clear:
**accountability is support, not enforcement.** The advisor asking about a prior commitment must do so in
a way that makes the person feel *cared for*, not *examined*. The specific risk with an anxious user:
if they didn't do the thing, asking about it can trigger shame — shame drives disengagement, not action.

The framing that works (practitioner consensus): **curiosity over accountability.** Not "did you do X?"
but "how did things go with X?" The first is a test; the second is an inquiry. If the answer is "I didn't
do it," the response is exploring what got in the way, not registering failure.

**Practitioner-only** for the specific framing. The underlying mechanism (shame → disengagement) is well-
supported in shame-resilience research (Brown, _Daring Greatly_, 2012 — commercial but grounded in
qualitative research).

### The concrete moves (texture)

**Helping an anxious user commit to one thing (end of thread):**
- **Evoke the commitment, don't assign it.** "What's the one thing you'd actually do before we next
  speak?" — their words, their choice, their motivation.
- **Make it implementation-intention shaped:** who/what/when/where. "Send an email" is weaker than
  "Send an email to [person] on [day], from my work account, before lunch." The more specific the if-then,
  the higher the completion.
- **Tie it to a value they already named.** "You said the thing you want most is to stop feeling like
  you're guessing — does this step get you closer to that?" (ACT: committed action is values-anchored.)
- **Keep it one thing.** Never a list. One small, completable action. This is already in the persona;
  now it is grounded in the Gollwitzer/ACT research.

**Following up on a prior commitment (returning user):**
- Open with curiosity, not a checklist: "How did things go with [X]?"
- If they did it: **genuine, specific affirmation** (MI — not praise inflation, a real acknowledgement of
  a real step toward their direction).
- If they didn't: explore without judgment. "What got in the way?" — this is MI sustain-talk exploration.
  The advisor's job is to understand the blocker, not to register a failure.
- If they don't know / have forgotten: hold it lightly. "It's fine — what's most useful to focus on today?"
  Never make the undone thing a bigger deal than the present session.

**The no-gamification form this takes:**
The mechanism is entirely relational — the advisor remembers, the advisor cares, the advisor asks. There
are no points, no streaks, no badges. The incentive is the relationship itself and the value of moving
forward, which is exactly what Kram's psychosocial functions describe. The external scaffold (the advisor
remembering) serves as temporary support toward **the user's own intrinsic momentum** — consistent with
the "scaffold to be internalised" principle already in the parent doc.

### Translation — what this means for a text-only AI advisor + our cohort

- **Implementation intentions are a perfect fit for text.** The advisor can ask the if-then question and
  reflect the answer back in an implementation-intention form: "So — on Thursday after work, you'll [X].
  I've got that." This is low-friction, feels like care not control, and is evidence-backed.
- **ACT defusion is a lighter lift in text than in therapy** — it doesn't require a full ACT protocol.
  The practical move: when a user says "I need to feel more confident before I do this," the advisor
  gently names it: "Sometimes the confidence comes after the first step, not before." This is the ACT
  move, text-native.
- **The follow-up question form matters enormously.** "Did you do it?" reads as cold and judgmental in
  text. "How did things go with [X]?" reads as warm inquiry. This is a one-line persona rule.
- **The anxious user who didn't do the thing will often not volunteer that.** The advisor must hold
  space for that disclosure — and make it safe to say "I didn't do it" without triggering shame and
  withdrawal. This is perhaps the most delicate behaviour in the whole follow-through arc.
- **Memory is the non-gamification mechanism.** The advisor remembers — not a streak counter, not a
  progress bar, just the advisor knowing what was agreed. This makes return visits meaningful in the way
  a good mentor would be meaningful: you come back and they remember where you were. This is already in
  the product direction; now it has research grounding.

  shape and the values tie to work as the research describes.

## Bucket I — The parking lot / bookmarking technique and structured follow-up

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. The parking lot as a standard facilitation technique — field consensus.**
The "parking lot" (also "idea bin," "bike rack," "issues bin") is a named technique in facilitation
practice — capturing tangential but valid contributions during a structured meeting or session so they
are not lost and not allowed to derail the current focus. Its use is described in virtually every
professional facilitation guide.

The standard form: the facilitator/mentor **visibly names the capture** ("I'm parking that — it's important
and I don't want to lose it") and **states the return intention** ("we'll come back to it [when]"). The
visibility of the capture is what makes it work — the person needs to see it acknowledged and held, or they
will keep raising it out of anxiety that it was dismissed.

**Practitioner-only** — this is field consensus with no single primary empirical study. It is the standard
tool in facilitation practice as described in IAF (International Association of Facilitators) resources
and the Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making (Kaner et al., 2007).
[IAF — International Association of Facilitators (facilitator tools)](https://www.iaf-world.org/) ·
_Cite as:_ Kaner, S. et al. (2007). _Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making_, 2nd ed.,
Jossey-Bass. (No open-access URL for book.)

**2. The return failure — the most common breakdown.**
Practitioners universally note that parking-lot items often simply do not get revisited. The failure modes:
- The session runs long and the parking lot is abandoned at the end.
- The item is parked but the mentor / facilitator has no mechanism to resurface it at the right moment.
- The person whose item was parked feels it was dismissed rather than deferred.

The prevention: **a committed return point at the moment of parking** — not "we'll come back to this"
(vague) but "we'll come back to this in about 20 minutes, after we've finished X" (specific). And:
**actually returning**, which requires the practitioner to actively track parked items rather than hoping
to remember. **Practitioner-only** — field consensus, no single study.

**3. GROW agenda-setting as the session-level parking mechanism.**
Whitmore's GROW model begins with a **Goal** question that includes agenda-setting: "What do you most want
to get from today's session?" This implicitly parks everything else for potential later. Experienced coaches
use a secondary form of this at mid-session: "We've covered [X] — there's also [Y] that you mentioned.
Which is more important for us to spend the remaining time on?" This is a form of real-time agenda
management that handles the competing-threads problem. **Verify-before-quoting** — this is practitioner
interpretation of GROW's Goal question; Whitmore does not specifically describe this as a parking technique.

**4. Follow-up as relational continuity, not task enforcement.**
The coaching literature (Clutterbuck, ICF competency framework) frames session-to-session follow-up as
**relational continuity** — the mentor brings what was agreed back into the conversation as an expression
of interest in the person's progress, not as auditing. ICF's Active Listening competency includes
"follows up on agreements" — it is listed as a dimension of listening quality, not a control mechanism.
**Verify-before-quoting** for the ICF framing; **framework-certain** that follow-up is a named mentoring
competency.
[ICF Core Competencies — Active Listening](https://coachingfederation.org/credentials-and-standards/core-competencies)

**5. The right return moment — timing, not just presence.**
Facilitation literature (Kaner et al.) notes that a parked item should be returned to when:
- The current thread has reached a natural conclusion or a decision point.
- The timing makes it relevant again (e.g. the user's situation has developed in a way that connects).
- There is enough time remaining to give it real attention.

Critically: **do not return to a parked item mid-thread** (while something else is live and unresolved).
This is the timing rule: parked items are revisited at the *seam* between topics, not inside one.
**Practitioner-only** — field consensus.

### The concrete moves (texture)

**At the moment of parking:**
- Acknowledge explicitly: "That's worth coming back to — I'm holding onto it."
- Name what you're parking: make it specific enough that both parties know what it refers to.
- Give a return signal: "Once we've finished [X], I'll bring it back."
- (In text: one sentence that does all three — "Worth holding onto — once we finish [X], I'll come back
  to [Y]. For now, [refocus on X].")

**At the return moment (after current thread resolves):**
- Surface it actively, don't wait for the user to ask: "Earlier you mentioned [Y] — do you want to pick
  that up now, or shall we save it for next time?"
- Offer both options: pick it up now, or explicitly schedule it for the next session. Either is fine; the
  key is that the person sees it was held.
- If time is short: "We're nearly at a stopping point — [Y] deserves more than a few minutes. Want to
  start there next time?"

**The failure mode to avoid:**
Parking an item and never returning to it — even once — destroys the trust in the mechanism. An anxious
user who raises something, sees it parked, and watches it never resurface will learn that "parking it" is
a polite way of dismissing it. One missed return corrupts the whole tool.

### Translation — what this means for a text-only AI advisor + our cohort

- **In text, the parking lot is entirely in state.** There is no physical whiteboard. The advisor's
  "park" is a stored note in the conversation state — and the discipline is surfacing it at the right
  seam. The system must be able to track: "user mentioned [X] — not pursued — return at next seam."
- **The return must be proactive, not user-prompted.** The whole value of the parking lot is that the
  person doesn't have to hold the item in their own head and remember to bring it back. If the advisor
  only returns to parked items when the user asks, the mechanism is broken — that's just the user
  managing their own agenda with extra steps.
- **Timing in text:** the "seam" between topics is when a thread reaches a natural conclusion — when
  the advisor has given the concrete next thing and the user has confirmed or responded to it. That is
  the moment to surface a parked item.
- **Our anxious cohort specifically benefits from the parking lot** because one of the hallmarks of
  anxiety is the fear that important things are being missed. The explicit "I'm holding onto [X]"
  acknowledgement reduces that ambient anxiety — it tells the person they don't have to hold all the
  threads simultaneously.
- **Session-end surfacing:** if a parked item hasn't been addressed by the end of a session, surface it
  explicitly: "We didn't get to [X] today — want to start there next time?" This gives it a home and
  closes the loop rather than letting it disappear into the session history.

### Check against what we already believe

- **CONFIRMS** the existing product-level parking lot practice (Lexi's idea-parking system) as a sound
  technique with a real facilitation rationale — the principle is the same.
- **FILLS A MAJOR GAP:** the advisor has no explicit parking-lot behaviour in the persona. This is new
  and needs adding: how to park (acknowledge, name, signal return), when to return (at the seam), and
  what to do if time runs out (park to next session explicitly).
- **REFINES:** the session-end behaviour in the persona ("always one concrete next thing") needs a
  partner rule: "surface any unaddressed parked items before closing, and give each a clear home."
- **NEW PRODUCT IMPLICATION:** the parking lot requires state that persists within a session (and
  potentially across sessions for bigger items). This is a light-weight but specific state-tracking need
  — the advisor must store parked topics and their intended return point. Flag for REBUILD.md.

## The central tension resolved — when to hold vs when to follow

_This is the question the research must answer. Here is the synthesised resolution._

### The principle (sourced)

The literature does not give a bright line — and honest practitioners say so. The GROW model is "not
followed slavishly." MI's rolling-with-resistance explicitly moves with the person. Clutterbuck's model
trusts that direction emerges through exploration. And yet: a mentor who only follows never drives anything
done (the accountability and follow-through literature is clear on this).

**The resolution the literature offers:** the mentor holds the thread **relationally, not structurally.**
This means:
- The mentor always knows where the conversation was and what was being worked on (structural tracking).
- The mentor never *forces* the conversation back to that point (structural control — wrong).
- The mentor *offers* the thread back when appropriate, as a genuine option — not an imposition (relational offering — right).
- The person chooses. The mentor honours the choice and holds the parked alternative for later.

**When following the switch wins:**
- The new topic is specific, external, named with a reason ("I just heard back from [company]").
- The switch arrives at a natural break, not mid-commitment.
- The person's affect is purposeful/grounded, not scattered/avoidant.
- Or: the signal is ambiguous and the advisor cannot tell — in which case follow and hold the thread.

**When gently holding wins:**
- The switch arrives immediately after a commitment question or a point of challenge (timing signal).
- The new topic is vague and exploratory, with no clear urgency.
- The person has a pattern (across sessions) of switching at exactly these moments.
- The advisor has tried following this kind of switch before and the session ended without any concrete
  next thing.

**The safe default when unsure:** follow the switch but explicitly hold the thread. Acknowledge both. Let
the person choose. This is always the psychologically safe move — it costs almost nothing if the switch
was legitimate, and it gently names the thread without pressure if it was avoidance.

### The avoidance-detection heuristic in text (synthesis)

No body language. No tone. Four signals available:

| Signal | Avoidance indicator | Genuine-need indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Arrives right after a commitment/challenge question | Arrives at a natural break |
| Specificity | Vague, diffuse new topic | Concrete, named, external new topic |
| Reason given | No reason offered | Reason is clear and stated |
| Session pattern | Recurs at the same kind of moment | Unusual for this person |

Default when ambiguous: ask gently ("what's making [X] feel more urgent right now?") before assuming
either way.

## Known gaps and lower-confidence areas

_Same standard as the parent doc — omissions are visible, not silent._

1. **No empirical study tested a text-based AI running these specific techniques with an anxious cohort.**
   Every translation in this document is reasoned from human-to-human evidence. The techniques are
   grounded; the transfer is a hypothesis. Same gap as the parent doc, applies doubly here because
   topic-switching and avoidance detection are even more dependent on interpersonal signals that don't
   survive text.

2. **The avoidance-detection heuristic (timing / specificity / reason / pattern) is synthesised, not
   sourced.** It is derived from MI sustain-talk theory + Pychyl's avoidance model + practitioner
   coaching doctrine. No single study validated this four-signal heuristic as a reliable tool. It is
   the best available reasoning given the evidence; treat it as a design principle, not a clinical
   instrument.

3. **ACT application to career coaching is not a clinical trial.** ACT has a strong evidence base in
   clinical psychology; its application to non-clinical coaching (values-anchored committed action for
   follow-through) is well-accepted in the practitioner community but less studied in a career-coaching
   RCT. Flag as "evidence-based mechanism, coaching application practitioner-confirmed."

4. **The parking-lot technique has no single primary study.** It is universal facilitation practice,
   described across IAF, Kaner, EMCC, and coaching guides. The mechanism (visibility of capture +
   committed return) is intuitively sound and practitioner-confirmed. We cannot cite a study that
   "proves" it works.

5. **"Shame → disengagement" for anxious users** — referenced in the follow-through section as a
   reason to avoid "did you do X?" framing. The direction is well-supported (Brown's shame research;
   Edmondson's psychological safety work); the precise magnitude and applicability to a career-coaching
   text chat is not established by a study. Treat as strong reasoning, not evidence.

6. **Matthews (2014) limits** — already flagged in parent doc. Repeated here because it is the follow-
   through accountability anchor. Do not over-claim.

7. **Pattern recognition across sessions (the "avoidance pattern" feature idea)** — identified here as
   a genuine capability advantage for an AI advisor. There is no study on whether surfacing a pattern to
   a user ("I notice this tends to come up when we're at a commitment question") is helpful or harmful
   in an anxious cohort. It could be illuminating; it could feel exposing. Would need a qualified
   practitioner to advise on the framing before building. *

## Where this leaves us — the behaviours to add

**Four specific behaviours this research adds to the advisor:**

1. **Hold-the-thread response:** acknowledge switch → reflect new topic briefly → offer a choice → bookmark
   original. Never ignore a switch; never immediately redirect.

2. **Avoidance-detection posture:** read the timing, specificity, and reason of the switch. When ambiguous,
   ask gently before assuming. Never label avoidance directly — name the pattern, not the pathology.

3. **Implementation-intention close:** when ending any thread, ask for a specific if-then commitment
   (when / where / what exactly?). Tie it to a named value. One thing only. Follow up with
   "how did things go?" not "did you do it?"

4. **Parking-lot discipline:** visibly acknowledge and name parked items at the moment of parking. Return
   to them proactively at the seam between topics — don't wait for the user to ask. Surface any unaddressed
   parking-lot items before session close and give each a home (now or next time).

**Two items for ADVISOR_PERSONA.md:**
- Add "how did things go with X?" as the follow-up form; explicitly ban "did you do X?"
- Add the hold-the-thread response sequence for topic switches.

**One item for REBUILD.md / engineering:**
- The parking-lot mechanism requires state tracking within and across sessions. Parked topics + their
  intended return point must be stored, not held in the prompt context only.

**One item for FEATURE-ROADMAP.md:**
- Pattern recognition across sessions: if a user consistently switches topic at commitment points, the

## Supplement — second research pass (2026-06-29, corroboration + additions)

_A parallel pass confirmed the four buckets above and added the following. Net-new items only; the caveats matter._

- **The advisor's OWN trap — the "righting reflex"** (Miller & Rollnick): the urge to correct/redirect/drag the
  person back to the "important" topic. It provokes reactance and more sustain talk. The rule: resist the righting
  reflex, but don't ignore sustain talk — reflect the ambivalence instead ("part of you wants to focus on X, part
  is drawn to Y — what's pulling you to Y?"). [framework-certain]
- **Sustain-talk vs change-talk is a LINGUISTIC code (DARN-CAT)** — designed to read motivation from *words*, so it
  transfers to text better than most. Switch + sustain talk about the original ("it's too early to think about
  that", "I'll get to it") = ambivalence/avoidance; switch + change talk about the new ("I actually need to…",
  "this matters because…") = likelier genuine priority. Sharpens Bucket G's heuristic. [framework-certain]
- **Implementation intentions work *because anxiety disrupts executive function* at the moment of action**
  (Achtziger/Parks-Stamm/Gollwitzer) — the if-then plan delegates initiation to a cue, removing the deliberation
  burden exactly when an anxious user can't deliberate. Strong grounding for our implementation-intention close.
  [framework-certain; the d≈0.65 effect is verify-before-quoting]
- **ACT framing for returning to an unfinished task** (Hayes et al.): action proceeds *despite* anxiety, not after
  it clears — and unfinished work is framed values-first ("this is still here because it still matters to you"),
  **never** as failure needing accountability. In tough weeks, committed action can shift to rest/self-compassion,
  not productivity. This is the research-grounded anti-gamification stance for the follow-up voice. [framework-certain
  for ACT; coaching application is practitioner-confirmed]
- **Self-forgiveness reduces future procrastination** (Pychyl/Wohl) — shame increases it. Reinforces "how did it
  go?" over "did you do it?" and "no mention of the gap". [framework-certain, direction; magnitude unestablished]

### Two CAVEATS that correct popular misattributions (do not repeat the myths)
- **Matthews (2014) is NOT a Harvard study** — it's a Dominican University conference presentation (ATINER 2015),
  not peer-reviewed, self-report, convenience sample. Directionally consistent with goal-setting literature; cite
  with the caveat, never as "a Harvard study". (Matches the parent doc's existing caution.) [verify-before-quoting]
- **The Zeigarnik Effect is overstated.** A 2025 review found no universal memory advantage for unfinished tasks;
  the better-supported finding is the **Ovsiankina effect** — a *tendency to resume* interrupted tasks. So frame a
  parked thread as "this is still with you" (resumption), not "you'll remember it because it's unfinished".
  [Ovsiankina = better-supported; Zeigarnik popular claim = do not use]
