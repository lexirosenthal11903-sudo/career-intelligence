## Cross-cutting principles (confirmed with Lexi, 2026-06-24)

_These emerged in discussion and cut across every bucket. They shape the whole session arc, not one part._

### 1. Adaptive mentorship — the advisor reads where you are and calibrates

Good mentors do **not** run the same session for everyone. There's an established **directive ↔ non-directive**
spectrum: directive = advice, instruction, suggestions ("here's what to do"); non-directive = facilitation,
drawing out what the person already has. A skilled mentor **moves along the spectrum based on the mentee's
needs** — instruction when they're lost, facilitation when they're capable — and switches "flexibly,
responsively, with regular check-ins," ideally with the mentee's awareness. _(Coaching Leaders; NHS NES; the
Push-Pull model, PMC.)_

**For us — this resolves the "spectrum of users" question:**
- **The lost user** (no direction, low motivation) → more non-directive _and_ more support: slow down, explore,
  full discovery arc, roles much later.
- **The directed user** ("I know roughly what I want — make me the best candidate") → lighter mentorship, faster
  to utility: less hand-holding, quicker to CV-tailoring and roles.
- **It's one adaptive advisor, not two products.** The opening "contracting" moment is where the advisor
  **diagnoses** — clear direction or not, high or low motivation — and that diagnosis sets _both_ how much
  hand-holding to apply _and_ how soon roles appear. Build the discovery path first (the differentiator), but
  design the arc as the "full" setting of a dial, never the only setting. Designing only for the lost user would
  bake in step-by-step that frustrates the directed user and have to be ripped out.

### 2. Momentum without pressure — things actually have to get done

A mentor who only explores and never drives action is useless. But momentum must clear our **no-gamification**
line (no streaks/points/badges — wrong register for an anxious user). The research gives the credible,
non-gamified mechanism:
- **Accountability is support and awareness, not enforcement.** Done well, the person "feels supported rather
  than pressured" — it drives follow-through without humiliation. _(CoachVantage; Bailey Balfour.)_
- **It's a scaffold to be internalised.** External accountability (the advisor) is "a temporary scaffold" that
  shifts the person from _extrinsic_ to _intrinsic_ motivation — the goal is the user's **own** momentum, not
  dependence on the tool. This is the answer to "how do you motivate without gamifying": you **evoke the
  person's own motivation** (see Bucket B — motivational interviewing) and scaffold it, you don't bolt on points.
- **Check-ins create momentum.** Regular, low-pressure "what did you do toward your direction?" check-ins prevent
  procrastination and build progress. (Pairs with Clutterbuck's progress-review question in Bucket A.) _A
  widely-cited figure puts goal completion far higher with a committed check-in partner — treat as indicative,
  not hard evidence._

**For us:** momentum = one concrete next action + the advisor remembering and checking back, framed as care not
pressure. It's already half-built into the persona ("stop thinking, do one thing"); now it's grounded and named
as a non-negotiable of the arc. Never metrics, never streaks — momentum comes from value and the relationship.

### 3. Roles are earned into view, not the front door

Roles should not be the landing surface. They're surfaced when direction has been explored, when the user asks,
or when the diagnosis says the user is ready (the directed user reaches them sooner). The advisor _brings_ you to
roles; they aren't the first thing you see. _(Follows directly from Bucket A's "don't prescribe before discovery.")_

## Bucket A — How mentors open and run a session (structure)

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. A mentoring relationship moves through phases — it isn't one flat conversation.**
David Clutterbuck (the leading UK mentoring authority) models the lifecycle in five phases:
1. **Rapport building** — mentor and mentee decide they want to work together and agree what each expects.
2. **Direction setting** — getting clear on what the mentee wants to achieve, and how.
3. **Progress making** — supporting committed change over time.
4. **Winding down** — outcomes delivered, or the mentee outgrows the mentor.
5. **Moving on** — the relationship becomes a lighter, ongoing connection.
Each phase needs different behaviour from the mentor. _(Clutterbuck mentoring lifecycle; NIHR phases of the
mentoring relationship.)_

**2. The first session is "contracting" — not content.**
Best practice across mentoring programmes: open a new relationship with an explicit expectation-setting
("contracting") stage that establishes the **purpose, aims, style and boundaries** of the relationship. Its
dual job is to set shared expectations _and_ create openness and trust. Rapport comes **before** diving into
problems — "dedicate most of the first meeting to getting to know each other," because people can't be open
before they trust you. _(Future Leaders Fellows Dev Network; multiple programme best-practice guides.)_

**3. Within a session, GROW is the standard spine.**
Sir John Whitmore's GROW model (_Coaching for Performance_, 1992) structures a session as
**Goal → Reality → Options → Will** (the will/commitment to act). Whitmore was explicit that it should
**not be followed slavishly** — a good conversation moves between the four at will. It's an awareness of
which _kind_ of question is most useful right now, not a script. _(Performance Consultants; Whitmore.)_

**4. ⚠️ The most important finding for us: do NOT pin down a goal too quickly.**
Clutterbuck CMI, directly on the mentee who arrives without clear goals: "Having a very specific goal at the
beginning is not necessarily helpful — indeed, it can be harmful. People often use coaching and mentoring to
**work out what their goals really are.**" The mentor explores from a **wide horizon** and **supports
discovery rather than prescription** — career vision emerges through deeper self-understanding.
_(Clutterbuck CMI — "What to do when the mentee has no clear career goals.")_

### The concrete moves (texture)

- **Open by contracting, warmly:** explain how this works, what it can do for them, what to expect — and
  build rapport — _before_ any problem-solving.
- **For the user with no goal, the mentor's actual techniques:**
  - **Career-pathing** — look back at past choices: "Was the route taken a considered choice, or did it just
    happen? Did you generate alternatives? Whose advice did you seek — and in retrospect, was it good?"
  - **Clarify contribution** — near-term unique value, and the longer-term causes they care about.
  - **Redefine progress** — personal metrics (learning, scope, satisfaction), not just climbing a ladder.
  - **Broaden horizons** — expose them to "people from different worlds of work" to show how their existing
    strengths transfer.
- **Hold direction open** — give clarity about _general direction and what they want from work_ (strengths,
  growth, values, purpose), not a locked job title.

### Translation — what this means for a chat advisor + our cohort

- **This validates the session order Lexi wants.** The researched arc is literally: contracting (how this
  works) → rapport → direction-setting (held open) → progress over time. **Roles-first is the textbook
  anti-pattern** — "don't pin the goal too quickly." Leading with a job list is prescription before discovery.
- **The advisor opening the session IS the "contracting" move** — and it's exactly what's missing today
  (reads as "dashboard + chatbot"). The fix for Step 1 is a real opening: how this works → what it'll help you
  achieve → the trajectory of support → _then_ explore.
- **Our product lifecycle ≈ Clutterbuck's phases.** Onboarding = rapport + direction-setting; daily companion
  = progress-making. We can borrow the phase model wholesale to know how the advisor's behaviour should change
  over the relationship, not just within one chat.
- **GROW gives the within-session spine — but weight "Goal" lightly for our user.** Hold it open; lead with
  Reality (who they are / what they've done) and exploration. Don't force a Goal in session one.
- **Career-pathing transfers well to chat** — "was that a real choice or did it just happen?" is a perfect
  advisor question for a CV/background we've just read. It's specific, it's about _them_, it isn't a form.

### Check against what we already believe

- **CONFIRMS Meraki** (receptive, listens-first input phase) = rapport/contracting. We had the instinct; the
  research names and backs it.
- **CONFIRMS "directions worth exploring," never "your direction is X"** = "don't pin the goal too quickly."
  Our locked direction-framing is on solid, sourced ground.
- **FILLS A GAP:** we have no explicit _contracting opener_. The persona's "opening move" is a single line;
  real contracting is richer — purpose, what to expect, boundaries, trajectory. **This is the core of the
  Step 1 rebuild.**
- **CHALLENGES nothing so far** — alignment is strong. Good sign our instincts were right; now they're grounded.

## Bucket B — How mentors speak (communication)

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. Active listening (Carl Rogers) — the foundation, decades of evidence.**
Rogers established that **reflecting feeling and meaning back** to a person — paraphrasing, confirming you've
understood — creates trust, validation and deeper self-insight. People who feel genuinely heard feel accepted,
and only then open up. The listener's job is to understand and reflect, not to redirect. _(Rogers & Farson,
_Active Listening_; EBSCO research starter.)_

**2. Motivational Interviewing — OARS (Miller & Rollnick) — strong evidence base.**
MI is the most evidence-backed method for **evoking a person's own motivation** (built in health behaviour
change, used far beyond it). Its core skills are **OARS**, used "early and often":
- **O — Open questions:** invite the person to tell their story in their own words, without leading them.
- **A — Affirmations:** genuine recognition of a real strength or a real step toward change, "no matter how big
  or small." Builds the person's confidence that they _can_ change. **This is not praise or cheerleading — it's
  specific and true.**
- **R — Reflections:** careful listening, then repeating / rephrasing / offering a deeper guess at what they mean.
- **S — Summaries:** gathering what you've heard back in an organised way, so they can reflect on its significance.

**3. The mechanism that matters: evoke "change talk."**
In MI, **any talk from the person about their own change predicts actual change.** The mentor's job is to use
OARS to draw out the person's _own_ reasons for moving — not to supply the motivation from outside. This is the
evidence-based version of "momentum without pressure" from the cross-cutting principles.

### The concrete moves (texture)

- **Lead with open questions, almost never closed ones.** "Tell me about what you've been doing" — not "Do you
  want a marketing job?" Discovery runs on open questions.
- **Reflect back, in their words, slightly deeper:** "What I'm hearing is the work you liked best had people in
  it, not spreadsheets." Specific to them, never generic.
- **Affirm real things:** "You taught yourself that — that's not nothing." Recognition of a true strength/step,
  never inflated praise.
- **Summarise at the turns:** "So — here's what I've got so far about you…" This organises and proves you heard.
- **Evoke, don't impose, motivation:** ask the question that gets _them_ to say why it matters, then reflect it back.

### Translation — what this means for a chat advisor + our cohort

- **MI is the engine for "motivate without gamifying."** You don't push an anxious user; you ask the open
  question that surfaces _their_ reason, reflect it, and that's intrinsic motivation — sourced, not bolted-on.
- **Reflections transfer perfectly to text** and they ARE our existing "reflects back what the user actually
  said, specific not generic" rule — now grounded in Rogers + MI. The advisor reflecting a CV back ("the thread
  through all of this is…") is a reflection.
- **Summaries are already a product moment for us** — the onboarding-bridge read and the recap card are
  literally MI summaries ("here's what I'm hearing about you"). Grounded.
- **⚠️ This refines our anti-cheerleading rule.** We currently lump _all_ affirmation in with banned cheerleading
  ("Amazing!", "You've got this!"). That's too broad. MI shows **genuine, specific affirmation is a core,
  evidence-based mentor skill** and we _should_ use it. The line is: **specific + true = good (use it); generic +
  inflating = banned.** "You taught yourself to code while working full time" ✓. "You're amazing!" ✗.
- **Medium caution:** human active-listening tools like minimal encouragers ("mm, go on", "I see") and silence
  don't survive text well — in chat they read as filler or a dead pause. The text-native equivalent: instead of
  "go on", give a real reflection or a real next question; "silence" = the advisor **not over-responding to every
  message** (already our "proactive but not overbearing" rule).

### Check against what we already believe

- **CONFIRMS** "reflects back what the user actually said," "never tells — shows, asks, waits," first-person warmth,
  and "proactive but not overbearing." Our voice instincts are well-aligned with Rogers + MI.
- **REFINES (important):** the anti-cheerleading rule is too blunt. Affirmation ≠ cheerleading. Keep banning empty
  praise; explicitly _permit and encourage_ specific, true affirmation. This needs to change in ADVISOR_PERSONA.md.
- **FILLS GAPS:** "open questions over closed" and "summarise at the turns" aren't named techniques in the persona
  yet. Add both.

## Bucket C — What mentors guide people through (the functions)

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. Kram's mentoring functions — THE foundational framework (Kathy Kram, 1985).**
From in-depth interviews with 18 senior/junior manager pairs; every established measure of mentoring is built on
it. Mentoring delivers **two kinds of function**:

- **Career functions** — advance the person's progress:
  **sponsorship · exposure and visibility · coaching · protection · challenging assignments.**
- **Psychosocial functions** — build the person's sense of competence, identity and confidence:
  **role modelling · acceptance and confirmation · counselling / emotional support · friendship.**

The headline: **real mentoring is _both_.** A relationship that only does career functions is transactional; the
psychosocial half is what makes it mentoring. _(Kram, _Mentoring at Work_, 1985; Univ. of Illinois mentoring
functions; IResearchNet.)_

**2. Strengths-based development (Gallup CliftonStrengths) — large evidence base.**
Core finding: human potential comes from **amplifying existing strengths, not eradicating weaknesses** —
"by investing in a weakness, the best you'll get to is average." Strong evidence on engagement, retention and
performance; people _aware of their strengths_ are far more likely to be flourishing. _(Gallup — figures are
indicative; verify before quoting.)_

### Translation — what this means for us (this bucket maps onto the product)

**Kram's two halves map almost one-to-one onto our roadmap — and prove "the advisor is the product":**

| Kram function | Our feature |
|---|---|
| **Coaching** | CV tailoring, cover letters, interview prep, "why am I not hearing back?" diagnosis |
| **Protection** | honest matching, the seniority ceiling — don't waste them on bad-fit / above-level roles |
| **Sponsorship / exposure** | networking & outreach / warm intros |
| **Challenging assignments** | "worth building" skills, direction stretch |
| **Role modelling** | the advisor's worldly, been-there persona |
| **Acceptance & confirmation** | "directions worth exploring," genuine affirmation |
| **Counselling / emotional support** | holding the anxiety (but NOT therapy — see Bucket D boundary) |
| **Friendship** | the daily companion, continuity, memory |

- **This validates "the advisor is the product, not a job board."** Psychosocial functions are literally _half_ of
  what mentoring is — and they're the half every other job tool skips. Our differentiator is doing both.
- **Our networking feature isn't a bolt-on** — it's the sponsorship/exposure function, a core part of mentoring.
- **⚠️ Honest medium limit:** a digital advisor **cannot truly sponsor** — it can't phone a hiring manager and
  vouch for you. So sponsorship/exposure only _partly_ transfers: we **equip the user to do their own outreach**
  (drafted intros, who to contact) rather than the advisor sponsoring directly. Be honest about this — don't imply
  the product can open doors it can't.
- **Strengths-based grounds our "trajectory, not deficit" rule** — lead from strengths. **But** balance it: pure
  strengths-only ignores real gaps (ATS, missing requirements) that genuinely block the goal. The honest version
  is what we already have — lead from strengths (psychosocial confidence) _and_ name the real gaps that matter
  (career function / honest matching). "Before you apply / worth building" is exactly this balance.

### Check against what we already believe

- **CONFIRMS "the advisor is half the product"** — psychosocial functions are half of mentoring, by the founding
  framework. Strong validation.
- **CONFIRMS** trajectory-not-deficit (strengths-based), honest matching (protection), and the networking feature
  (sponsorship) as a _core mentoring function_, not an add-on.
- **FILLS A GAP — useful tool:** Kram's function list is a **checklist to audit the product against.** We're
  strong on coaching + protection; weaker (by medium) on sponsorship; the psychosocial side lives almost entirely
  in the advisor's voice, so that voice has to carry real weight.
- **CAUTION:** sponsorship doesn't fully survive an AI medium — keep the product honest about what it can and can't do.

## Bucket D — The relationship foundation: safety, trust, boundaries (lighter)

### The factual structure (sourced)

**1. Psychological safety (Amy Edmondson, Harvard — heavily researched).**
Defined as "a belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns,
or mistakes." It's what makes **interpersonal risk-taking** — asking for help, admitting you don't know — feel
safe. Built by **modelling humility and, crucially, curiosity** — "dropping judgment, embracing a beginner's mind."
_(Edmondson; HBS.)_

**2. Mentoring boundaries — what a mentor must NOT do.**
- **A mentor is not a therapist.** They offer "perspective from professional experience, not clinical mental
  health support." A sounding board, yes; counselling/diagnosis, no.
- **Operate within competence; refer on when exceeded.** "If competence is exceeded, you refer the mentee on
  [to a therapist/counsellor] and you stop mentoring them" on that issue. Mentoring "is not appropriate for
  addressing issues of mental health."
- **Avoid dependency** — the mentee coming to "rely unduly on the relationship to meet an unconscious need."
- **Boundaries are set by defining purpose/scope upfront** — i.e. the same contracting move as Bucket A.
_(Mentorloop; Amanda Dudman; CoachMentoring.co.uk.)_

### Translation — what this means for us

- **Psychological safety is THE foundation for our cohort.** Our user has to feel safe to say "I have no idea what
  I want," "my CV is thin," "I've applied to 100 things and heard nothing." The advisor creates that with
  **curiosity, never judgment** — never makes them feel behind. This grounds "no mention of the gap," "welcome
  back," and "trajectory not deficit." It's also the rule for thin-input probing: it must feel *safe and curious*,
  never like a quiz or an exam.
- **Dependency-avoidance reinforces the scaffold principle** — the advisor builds the user's *own* momentum and
  agency, it doesn't engineer compulsive reliance. Another reason **no gamification** is correct.
- **Boundaries-via-contracting** is one more reason the opening matters: it quietly sets scope ("I help you find a
  direction and get hired — I'm not a counsellor").

### ⚠️ Check against what we already believe — one CRITICAL gap

- **CONFIRMS** "not a therapy tool — holds space but doesn't diagnose," "no mention of the gap," trajectory-not-
  deficit, the scaffold/no-dependency principle, no-gamification. All well-aligned.
- **FILLS A CRITICAL SAFETY GAP (flag to Lexi):** the persona says the advisor "holds space but does not diagnose
  or counsel" — but it **never defines what the advisor DOES when a user is in genuine distress.** Our product
  deliberately targets *anxious* early-career people; some **will** disclose real mental-health crises (despair,
  hopelessness, self-harm ideation). The mentoring literature is unambiguous: that exceeds scope → the mentor must
  **gently name the limit and signpost to real support, not play therapist.** We need:
  1. A defined **escalation/signposting behaviour** in ADVISOR_PERSONA.md — when distress crosses a line, the
     advisor warmly acknowledges, states it's beyond what it can help with, and points to real help (e.g. in the
     UK: Samaritans 116 123, the user's GP, university counselling). Never diagnoses, never tries to treat.
  2. A **product/legal safeguarding note** (duty of care) — likely a line in terms + a crisis-signpost surface.
     **Pre-launch non-negotiable, Lexi to action** — sits with the ICO/privacy items.

## Bucket E — Does mentoring actually work? (the evidence / credibility layer)

_This is the bucket we'd quote externally, so the two headline findings below are **verified against the primary
sources** (PubMed / open-access PMC), not blog summaries._

### The evidence (verified)

**1. Allen, Eby, Poteet, Lentz & Lima (2004), _Journal of Applied Psychology_ 89(1), 127–136 — "Career Benefits
Associated With Mentoring for Protégés: A Meta-Analysis."** The first meta-analysis of protégé career outcomes.
Findings were **generally supportive** of mentoring's benefits across both objective (e.g. compensation) and
subjective (e.g. career satisfaction) outcomes — but **effect sizes for objective outcomes were small.** 43 studies.

**2. Eby, Allen, Evans, Ng & DuBois (2008), _Journal of Vocational Behavior_ 72(2), 254–267 — "Does Mentoring
Matter? A Multidisciplinary Meta-Analysis."** Synthesised youth, academic and workplace mentoring across **six
outcome domains** — behavioral, attitudinal, health-related, relational, motivational, and career. Verified
conclusion, quoted accurately:
> "Mentoring is significantly correlated in a favorable direction with a wide range of protégé outcomes" but
> "the overall magnitude of association between mentoring and outcomes was small in magnitude."

Effect sizes were consistently small (Cohen's ~.10–.23, some medium). The authors explicitly warn practitioners to
**"temper… sometimes seemingly unrealistic expectations about what mentoring can offer."** Notably, **academic and
workplace mentoring showed larger effects than youth mentoring** — and our product is squarely workplace/career,
the stronger-evidence category.

### Translation — what we can honestly claim (and what we can't)

- **The honest, credible posture — which is also the _stronger_ posture:** mentoring is evidenced to help across a
  **wide range** of outcomes (career, motivational, health, attitudinal — note: the psychosocial side, not just
  pay), **but the effects are modest, not transformational.** We do **not** claim "mentoring transforms your
  career" or attach a dramatic number. Honesty here is a credibility asset, not a weakness — and it matches our
  whole "honest matching" identity.
- **No evidenced outcome claims — we have no outcome data (Lexi, 2026-06-24).** We never state getting-hired-faster
  as a _result_, never a success rate, never "we transform careers." **The one claim we make is a help + mechanism
  claim:** _we help you get hired faster by making each application more meaningful and targeted_ — fewer, stronger,
  better-targeted applications. The value is the mechanism, not a promised outcome.
- **What we say to a university / sceptic:** "The advisor is built on the established frameworks the field runs on
  — Kram's mentoring functions, motivational interviewing, Rogers' active listening, Clutterbuck's developmental
  model — and mentoring is evidenced across multiple meta-analyses to benefit people across many domains (real if
  modest effects), with the strongest evidence in the workplace/career context we operate in."
- **Do not repeat the 65%/95% goal-completion statistic publicly** — unverified, frequently mangled. The two
  meta-analyses above are the citations we stand on.

### Check against what we already believe

- **CONFIRMS** the no-hype, honest register the whole product is built on. The evidence rewards honesty.
- **FILLS A GAP:** we now have **two real, verified, citable sources** for "mentoring works" — the spine of the
  credibility framework the parked mentorship-strategy session needs.

**The throughline:** a good mentor **opens by orienting** (contracting — how this works, what it'll do for you),
**reads where you are** and calibrates (directive↔non-directive), makes it **safe** to not-know (psychological
safety, curiosity not judgment), **explores before prescribing** (never pins a goal early — roles come later),
**speaks** by listening-and-reflecting and **evoking your own motivation** (active listening + MI/OARS),
delivers **both career and psychosocial functions** (Kram — the psychosocial half is our differentiator),
drives **momentum as supportive scaffolding** toward your own agency (not gamification), and **stays in its lane**
(not a therapist — signposts real help when needed). The evidence says this helps, modestly but really — so we
claim it honestly.

   safeguarding/signpost behaviour).

   → roles later), adaptive by user type, for the next build session to implement.
