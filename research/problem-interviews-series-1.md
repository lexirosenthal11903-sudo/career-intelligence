## Interview 1 — Yifal

*PPE graduate · July 2024 · Pursuing political comms and public affairs*

Yifal knew she wanted 'something political' but had no map for what that meant in practice. Direction emerged through accidental exposure — a work experience that showed her the commercial side of politics — not through any system designed to help her navigate it. Even now, with a relatively clear target sector, she navigates primarily through a niche industry-specific site (W4MP) and direct LinkedIn company follows, having concluded early that mainstream job boards are not fit for purpose.

> *"LinkedIn — it needs to be advertised that it's bad."*
> — Yifal · Interview 1

> *"I did low-key get to a point where I was like, I'll take anything."*
> — Yifal · Interview 1 — on desperation mode

> *"I go through dips where I get really motivated and really demotivated."*
> — Yifal · Interview 1 — on motivation collapse

**Key unprompted moments:**
- Described LinkedIn as actively bad before being asked about it
- Named the motivation dip cycle — feast/famine engagement — as a lived pattern
- 'I'll take anything' came out when the platform concept was described, not in response to a direct question about desperation
- Identified face-to-face human connection as the real route — said twice unprompted
- Welcome to the Jungle rated positively specifically because it asked questions before suggesting roles — validates onboarding logic

## Interview 2 — UCL Finance Student

*MSc Accounting & Finance, UCL · History graduate (2:1) · Target-school, multiple internships, pursuing high finance / consulting*

This interviewee presents as highly strategic — target school, multiple internships, a clear industry — and still experiences the process as damaging and arbitrary. The silence between applications caused genuine psychological harm, not simply frustration. He described the job search as gambling: not as hyperbole, but as a considered observation about how the behavioural loop of applying, waiting, and hoping mirrors the psychology of addiction. He had thought about this independently before the conversation.

> *"The silence in between is sometimes very damaging because there's nothing much you can do."*
> — Interview 2

> *"You question your worth. You question — when am I going to start my life?"*
> — Interview 2 — on the silence between applications

> *"The behaviours of a job searcher is tantamount almost exactly to gamblers."*
> — Interview 2 — unprompted observation

> *"I don't think the internet can give you the same empathy as a human being."*
> — Interview 2 — on why people outperform platforms

**Key unprompted moments:**
- Gambling comparison — arrived at entirely independently, mid-flow
- 'When am I going to start my life?' — emerged spontaneously when describing the silence
- 140 cold calls over three and a half months — including through exams and a dissertation — one contact turned out to be a scam
- Named Claude by name as a compensatory tool he already uses for interview prep
- Warned the interviewer about the Mum Test at the end — unprompted, showing awareness of validation bias

## Interview 3 — Leah

*Graduate July 2025 · Administration at a fashion house · Volunteering as Met Police constable · Direction actively evolving*

Leah offers the starkest illustration of the desperate-rather-than-intentional pattern. She was not looking for a career when she graduated — she was looking for survival. She is currently on a fixed-term contract she knows is ending, is aware she should be searching, and is actively choosing not to because the process is 'so horrible.' She is using passive hope — that the contract will be extended — as a substitute for engagement. This is motivation collapse not as feeling but as behaviour: visible, concrete, and consequential.

> *"I wasn't really thinking about careers — I was thinking of any move I could get to be able to make money and sustain myself after uni."*
> — Leah · Interview 3

> *"I can't be bothered because the process is so horrible."*
> — Leah · Interview 3 — on why she is not currently searching

> *"I have like six CVs saved on my desktop."*
> — Leah · Interview 3 — her workaround for a broken system

**Key unprompted moments:**
- Survival mode stated before any question about career direction or desperation
- Active avoidance of job search described plainly — 'I can't be bothered because the process is so horrible'
- Six CVs on her desktop — engineered her own workaround, described matter-of-factly rather than as a complaint
- Instagram DM to a café owner outperformed every formal platform — she got an interview and a job offer
- Described LinkedIn as overwhelming, non-personal, and hard to feel 'inspired' by — that word choice is significant

## Pattern Analysis

**1. LinkedIn is broken and everyone knows it**
All three interviewees independently concluded that LinkedIn is not fit for purpose. Interview 1 said it needs to be 'advertised as bad.' Interview 2 described the experience as gambling. Interview 3 used the word 'non-personal' and said it was hard to feel inspired. No one defended it. The platform's positioning against job boards is not a differentiator — it is the baseline requirement.

**2. The silence is the specific wound**
All three named not hearing back — not rejection itself, but absence — as the most damaging part of the process. Interview 2 named it explicitly as causing psychological harm. Interview 3 said it made effort feel unreciprocated. Interview 1 described the motivation dips that follow from it. The silence is where confidence erodes. The platform replaces silence with signal, progress, and momentum — this is not a feature, it is the emotional core of what the product does.

**3. Human contact is understood to be the answer — but inaccessible**
All three arrived independently at the conclusion that direct human contact is the most effective route. Interview 1 attended events and networked. Interview 2 made 140 cold calls. Interview 3 sent an Instagram DM and got a job. None of them had a systematic, scalable way to do this. The contact discovery and personalised outreach features of the platform are not a nice-to-have — they are the answer to the question all three are already asking.

**4. Direction confusion exists even in people who appear to have a plan**
Interview 2 was the most strategically sophisticated candidate in the group — target school, multiple internships, a clear industry target. He still spent the first half of university not knowing what finance actually looked like in practice. Interview 1 had a vocational degree and a sector in mind, but no map for the roles within it. Interview 3 had abandoned direction entirely in favour of survival. In all three cases, clarity came through accidental exposure and access to networks — not through any system built to help them navigate.

**5. People are already compensating — with workarounds and AI tools**
All three interviewees had built their own compensatory systems: six saved CVs, Claude for interview prep, CV Wolf for keyword matching, niche sector-specific sites, Instagram DMs. They are doing the work the platform should be doing, manually and inefficiently. This is strong validation that the need is real — people are already trying to solve it. The question is whether the platform can solve it better.

---

## Hypothesis Assessment

After three interviews, the hypothesis is **early-stage validated** in its core claims. The three themes that appeared in every conversation — direction confusion, application silence, and motivation collapse — map directly onto the three problems the platform is built to solve. No interviewee contradicted the hypothesis. All three experienced the process as something that happened *to* them rather than something they could navigate with intention.

One finding worth watching: the avoidance behaviour surfaced in Interview 3 — a person who is not in crisis, not actively searching, but disengaged from the process entirely because it is too aversive. This may be a larger user cohort than expected: people who should be searching, know they should be searching, and are choosing not to. The platform needs to work for people who have already given up, not just people who are still trying.
