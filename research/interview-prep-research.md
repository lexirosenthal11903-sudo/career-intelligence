# PART 1 — PLANNING

## 1. The formats taxonomy — what you're actually walking into

The single most useful thing the advisor can do *before* any prep is establish **which format** the user is
facing, because preparation differs sharply by format. Most candidates over-index on "common interview
questions" and under-prepare for the actual structure. The UK early-career landscape has nine recognisable
formats.

**1.1 Screening / phone call (Tier A).** A short (15-30 min) first-stage call, usually from a recruiter or
talent team, not the hiring manager. Purpose: confirm basics (eligibility, availability, salary expectations,
motivation), filter out obvious non-fits, and check you can hold a conversation. Low on deep competency
probing; high on "why this role, why us, are you a safe pass-through." Often the gate to everything else.

**1.2 Competency / behavioural interview (Tier A).** The UK default. Questions ask for *past* evidence:
"Tell me about a time when…". The premise is that past behaviour predicts future behaviour. Answered with the
**STAR** structure (Section 2). Used across virtually every sector and the backbone of the Civil Service
process, NHS, large corporates, and most graduate schemes. _(National Careers Service: "The STAR method";
Prospects.ac.uk: "Competency-based interviews".)_

**1.3 Strengths-based interview (Tier A).** A meaningful and growing share of large UK graduate employers have
moved to strengths-based interviewing — EY runs its entire graduate process this way, and Cappfinity (Capp)
provides strengths assessments used by a number of large UK employers (per Cappfinity's own published client
material and employer careers pages — Tier B for the specific client list, which changes). Questions probe what
*energises* you and what you do naturally and well, not just what you've achieved: "What do you enjoy doing?
What comes easily to you? What does a good day look like?" They come faster, in greater number, and you can't
pre-script them. The premise: people perform best in roles aligned to their genuine strengths. _(ey.com:
"Strengths"; targetjobs: "EY strengths-based interview".)_

**1.4 Technical interview (Tier A for existence; Tier B for tactics).** Role-specific skill assessment. For
software roles: live coding / pair-programming, LeetCode-style problems via HackerRank or Codility, data
structures and algorithms, plus system-design at higher levels. For finance: numerical/markets questions. For
data: SQL/stats. Increasingly paired with a behavioural round in the same loop. _(GradSignal: "Graduate
software engineer interview questions"; Indeed UK: "Software engineer interview questions".)_

**1.5 Case interview (Tier A for existence; Tier B for tactics).** The defining format for management
consulting (MBB — McKinsey, BCG, Bain — and the Big Four consulting arms). You're given a business problem and
must structure an approach, do live arithmetic, and reach a recommendation, thinking out loud throughout. Some
firms (notably McKinsey) use interviewer-led cases; others candidate-led. _(managementconsulted.com;
igotanoffer.com: "Case interview frameworks".)_

**1.6 Panel interview (Tier A).** Several interviewers at once (common in public sector, NHS, academia,
charities, and final rounds generally). Each may own different criteria or behaviours. The challenge is
distributing eye contact and not freezing under multiple assessors. Civil Service final panels are a defined
example.

**1.7 Assessment centre (Tier A).** A half- or full-day event where 8-12 candidates complete multiple
exercises (group exercise, presentation, in-tray/e-tray, individual interview, sometimes a written exercise
or role-play) while assessors score against a competency framework. You are observed throughout, including in
breaks. Standard for large graduate schemes (PwC, NHS Graduate Management Training Scheme, Civil Service Fast
Stream). _(targetjobs: "Group exercises: what to expect"; graduatesfirst.com: "Assessment centre exercises".)_

**1.8 Presentation interview (Tier A).** You present on a set topic (given in advance or on the day), assessed
on communication, structure, and handling questions. Common in marketing, consulting, academia, and as one
station within an assessment centre.

**1.9 Asynchronous / AI video interview ("one-way") (Tier A that it's widespread; Tier B on the AI-scoring
detail).** You record answers to pre-set questions on camera, often with a time limit and limited retakes; no
live human. Used heavily at high-volume graduate intake to filter before live rounds. Increasingly screened
by AI (speech transcribed and run through a model for "competency fit"), though automated *scoring* of video is
the most contested use of AI in hiring and is criticised for embedded bias. Peer-reviewed evidence also
suggests one-way formats can deter candidates: 2025 research (one field study plus three controlled
experiments) found applicants report **lower job-acceptance intentions and lower psychological comfort** after
asynchronous video interviews than after face-to-face interviews, which in turn reduces their willingness to
self-disclose (Tier B — a single research programme, not yet replicated at UK-graduate-population scale; design
factors such as age and gender are an active research question). _(Institute for Employment Studies (IES):
"Artificial intelligence (AI) in the job interview process"; the REC: "Asynchronous video interview assessment
tool"; applicant-reactions research published in the European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology /
International Journal of Selection and Assessment, 2025.)_

**How the advisor establishes format:** the very first move in interview prep is to ask the user what stage and
format they're facing (or read it from the invitation if they paste it). If they don't know, the advisor infers
the *likely* format from employer + role + stage (e.g. "a graduate scheme at a Big Four firm → expect an
asynchronous video screen, then an assessment centre with a strengths interview").

## 2. Question types by format + the answer structures

### 2.1 Competency / behavioural — the STAR (or STARR) structure (Tier A)

The UK careers consensus (National Careers Service, Prospects, and most university careers services) is that
competency answers are built with **STAR**: **Situation** (briefly set the scene), **Task** (your specific
responsibility), **Action** (what *you* did — the longest part, "I" not "we"), **Result** (the outcome, ideally
tangible). Many UK services teach **STARR**, adding **Reflection** (what you learned / would do differently),
which suits strengths-laced competency questions and shows self-awareness. _(National Careers Service: "The
STAR method"; University of Glasgow Careers; University of Reading: "STAR technique".)_

**The preparation mechanic (Tier A):** read the job description, extract the 5-8 core competencies/behaviours
it asks for, and prepare **5-8 STARR stories** from your real experience that you can flex across competencies.
The same story (e.g. a group project that went wrong) can answer teamwork, conflict, leadership, or resilience
depending on which part you foreground. _(Prospects.ac.uk: "Competency-based interviews".)_

**The common failure modes (Tier A):** rambling with no structure; speaking in hypotheticals ("I *would*
usually…") instead of a real past example; describing the situation and task but never landing the *result* or
your specific contribution; saying "we" so the assessor can't tell what *you* did.

### 2.2 Strengths-based — why you can't script, and what to do instead (Tier A/B)

You cannot prepare set answers because the questions are numerous, fast, and about authentic preference, not
rehearsed achievement. What works (per EY's own guidance): **know yourself thoroughly** — your genuine
strengths, what energises you, concrete moments you were at your best — so you can respond fluidly and
honestly rather than reaching for a script. EY distinguishes **realised** strengths (things you clearly do
well and often) from **unrealised** strengths / **potential** (areas you could grow into). Authenticity is the
point: a strengths interview is partly designed to detect rehearsed, inauthentic answers. _(ey.com:
"Strengths"; targetjobs: "How to excel in a strengths-based graduate interview".)_

### 2.3 Technical — structure your thinking out loud (Tier B)

For coding: clarify the problem and edge cases before writing; talk through your approach; start with a
working solution then optimise; state time/space complexity. Interviewers in 2026 weigh **communication and
reasoning** heavily, not just whether you reach the answer — partly because behavioural signal predicts
on-team success and partly as a hedge against AI-assisted "perfect" silent answers. Build a story bank for the
behavioural half that almost always accompanies the technical half. _(GradSignal; Indeed UK; community
practitioner consensus — Tier B.)_

### 2.4 Case — structure beats cleverness (Tier B)

The recurring expert finding: **do not apply a canned framework mechanically.** Interviewers at MBB report
that the most common reason for a low score is "applied a generic framework without adapting it to the case."
The move: take 30-60 seconds to build a *bespoke* structure for *this* problem, open with a one-sentence
overview of your buckets, make it MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive), then work through it,
doing the arithmetic cleanly and stating a recommendation. Knowing the classic structures (profitability =
revenue − cost, market entry, the 3Cs/4Ps, pricing) is table stakes; *tailoring* them is the skill. Practice
volume matters — significant practice *out loud* over weeks, with a partner or the advisor's mock, is what
builds the fluency. (Commercial coaching sources cite specific figures like "30-50 cases," but they have an
incentive to inflate prep volume, so the exact number is unverified — see Known Gaps; the advisor should not
set a "you need 30-50 cases" expectation. Tier B.) _(managementconsulted.com; hackingthecaseinterview.com;
igotanoffer.com — directional only.)_

### 2.5 The named-employer behavioural systems (Tier A that they exist)

Two are common enough to name because the advisor can ground directly in the employer's *published*
material (this is the §Company-specific intelligence route — public, free, authoritative):

- **Civil Service Success Profiles (gov.uk, Tier A).** Recruitment assesses up to five elements: **Behaviours**
  (9 defined behaviours, a subset chosen per role and grade), **Strengths**, **Ability**, **Experience**, and
  **Technical**. The job advert tells you which behaviours are assessed; you prepare a STAR example for each
  named behaviour at the right grade level. Strengths are assessed separately and more spontaneously. _(GOV.UK:
  "Success Profiles: Civil Service behaviours"; "Success Profiles: Strengths".)_
- **Amazon Leadership Principles (Tier A that they're published).** Amazon interviews are explicitly structured
  around its 16 Leadership Principles; each interviewer is assigned specific principles, answers must use STAR
  with **quantified results** (an answer without metrics scores poorly), and a **Bar Raiser** (an experienced
  Amazonian outside the hiring team, with veto) probes deeply with follow-ups. Candidates prepare many distinct
  STAR stories mapped to the principles. _(Amazon published Leadership Principles; multiple consistent prep
  guides — Tier A for the framework's existence, Tier B for the "how many stories" specifics.)_

### 2.6 Assessment-centre exercises (Tier A/B)

- **Group exercise:** 6-8 candidates discuss a problem while assessors score communication, teamwork,
  listening, *building on others' ideas*, and influencing. The failure mode is trying to dominate or staying
  silent; the win is moving the group forward and bringing others in. _(targetjobs: "Group exercises".)_
- **In-tray / e-tray:** prioritise 15-20 items under time pressure; assessed on prioritisation, judgement,
  attention to detail. _(graduatesfirst.com.)_
- **Presentation:** structure, clarity, timekeeping, handling questions.
- **Individual interview within the centre:** usually competency-based as above.

the interview-side companion to the outreach sector matrix. Tiers reflect how well-sourced each row is.

|---|---|---|---|

## 4. How to prepare for each format — the real techniques

**Universal base (Tier A):** (1) Research the employer and role enough to answer "why this role, why us" with
something specific and true. (2) Re-read the job description and map its competencies/behaviours. (3) Build the
5-8 STARR story bank. (4) Prepare your own questions (Section 8). (5) Rehearse **out loud** — silent reading
is the most common and least effective prep (Section 5). _(National Careers Service; Prospects.)_

**Per format, the specific add-on:**
- **Screening call:** have a crisp 60-second "about me / why I'm here", know your salary expectation and
  availability, and treat it as real (people relax and fail the easy stage).
- **Competency:** the story bank, flexed to the named competencies; practise landing the *result*.
- **Strengths:** self-knowledge work, not scripts — list what energises you and concrete "at my best" moments;
  practise answering fast and honestly.
- **Technical:** drill the fundamentals + timed problems; practise narrating your reasoning; prepare the
  behavioural half too.
- **Case:** 20-30+ cases out loud with a partner (or the advisor's mock); structure and mental-maths drills.
- **Panel:** practise distributing attention; prepare for each panellist's likely angle.
- **Assessment centre:** practise a group exercise dynamic (contribute + include), an in-tray prioritisation,
  and a timed presentation; remember you're observed all day.
- **Presentation:** build a clear three-part structure, time it, rehearse the Q&A.
- **Asynchronous video:** practise to camera, look at the lens, prepare for limited retakes and a timer, sort
  lighting/background/connection in advance (Section 12); structure answers even though no one interrupts.

# PART 2 — THE LIVE MOCK INTERVIEW

## 5. What makes a mock actually work — and how the advisor runs one

**The evidence (Tier B, consistent direction).** Studies of mock interviews report two effects: **reduced
anxiety** and **improved performance**, driven by two ingredients — **realistic practice (exposure)** and
**specific feedback**. Reviewing a recorded performance amplifies the gain by letting candidates see and
correct their own errors. The effect is strongest when the practice resembles the real format and the feedback
is concrete and behavioural, not vague encouragement. _(Wilkie, L. & Rosendale, J. (2024), "Efficacy and
benefits of virtual mock interviews: analysing student perceptions of digital employment preparations,"
Journal of University Teaching and Learning Practice, 21(1) — a peer-reviewed student-perception study (not a
UK-population RCT); its finding: students judged mocks useful, and the strongest predictor of benefit was
their own preparedness beforehand. With the broader practice-and-feedback literature and UK careers-service
interview guides (Cambridge, Warwick, Loughborough) — Tier B.)_

**Why this matters for us specifically:** our user is anxious by definition. Exposure-based practice is also
the most evidence-based route to *lower* anxiety (Section 7). So the mock is doing double duty — it builds
competence *and* it is the anxiety intervention. That is a strong on-mission reason to build it well.

**How the advisor runs a mock (the loop):**
1. **Set up:** confirm the role, sector, and **format** (Section 1). Calibrate (Section 6).
2. **Frame it:** explain it'll ask questions one at a time, the user answers as they would in the real thing,
   and feedback comes after — warm, specific, honest.
3. **Ask in turn:** one question, wait for the full answer, don't coach mid-answer (that defeats the
   exposure). For competency, probe with a follow-up the way a real interviewer would ("what was *your* part
   in that?"). For strengths, go faster. For case/technical, let them work.
4. **Feedback after each (or in a batch):** behaviourally specific — what landed, what was missing
   (e.g. "strong situation, but I never heard the *result* — what changed because of what you did?"), one
   concrete fix. Never a score; never empty praise (no-gamification + honesty rules).
5. **Iterate:** offer a re-run of a weak answer so they feel the improvement — the exposure-plus-feedback loop
   the evidence rewards.
6. **Close:** name the two or three things to work on, and one genuine strength to keep.

**Pairs with voice (logged features):** the live mock is the strongest case for the voice-input and
voice-call features — answering *out loud* is exactly what the evidence says works, and silent typing is the
weakest rehearsal. The advisor should encourage speaking the answer even in text mode.

## 6. Calibrating the mock to interview type, and feedback that changes behaviour

**Calibration (what changes by format):**
- **Competency:** STAR-shaped questions, real follow-ups, feedback on structure and the missing *result/your
  role*.
- **Strengths:** rapid, energy-and-preference questions; feedback on authenticity and specificity, not polish.
- **Technical/case:** let them reason out loud; feedback on structure, clarity of thinking, and
  communication as much as the answer.
- **Screening:** quick-fire basics + motivation; feedback on crispness.
- **Panel/assessment centre:** simulate multiple angles; feedback on inclusion/influence for group dynamics.

**Feedback that changes behaviour (Tier B — practice-and-feedback literature + UK careers-service interview
guides, e.g. Cambridge, Warwick, Loughborough, which articulate this same specificity principle):** specific
beats global
("your action step was vague" beats "good job"); one or two priorities beat a list (cognitive load); pair
every gap with a concrete next move; and where possible let them *immediately re-attempt* so the correction is
felt, not just heard. The honest register matters: false reassurance ("that was perfect!") is both dishonest
and useless to an anxious user who needs to actually improve.

## 7. Interview anxiety — evidence-based management

Our cohort arrives anxious, so this is core, not a footnote. The evidence points to a small set of techniques
that genuinely help (Tier B — drawn from applied/sports psychology and careers-service guidance, consistent
across sources but not a single UK primary study):

- **Exposure through realistic practice is the most effective anxiety reducer** — which is precisely the mock.
  Over-preparation builds legitimate confidence that lowers anxiety at its source.
- **Reframe arousal, don't fight it.** The goal isn't to "calm down" but to relabel nerves as
  readiness/excitement. This is the best-evidenced item here: Alison Wood Brooks' "Get Excited: Reappraising
  Pre-Performance Anxiety as Excitement" (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014) showed across
  controlled studies (public speaking, singing, maths) that people who reappraised anxious arousal as
  excitement — via something as simple as saying "I am excited" — performed measurably better than those who
  tried to calm down. ("I'm nervous because this matters" beats "I need to not be nervous." Dated 2014 but a
  foundational, still-widely-cited finding — Tier A for the mechanism, Tier B for transfer to interviews
  specifically.)
- **Box breathing (4-4-4-4)** before the interview activates the parasympathetic ("rest and digest") system.
- **5-4-3-2-1 grounding** (name five things you see, four you hear…) interrupts a panic spiral; the technique
  is supported for acute anxiety (University of Rochester Medical Center).
- **Shift attention outward during the interview** — focus on what the interviewer is actually asking and how
  you can help, not on monitoring yourself. Self-monitoring fuels self-consciousness; outward focus reduces it.

_(Sources: Brooks 2014 (arousal reappraisal, above); University of Rochester Medical Center on 5-4-3-2-1
grounding; careers-service and applied-psychology consensus on exposure, breathing and outward focus. Quote
the techniques, not specific effect sizes.)_

**Voice / register rules for the advisor here:** validate the feeling briefly and move to a concrete action
(the spiraller pattern — stop adding information, give one next step). Never minimise ("don't worry!"), never
catastrophise, never over-narrate its own caveats. This is the emotional arc made real: Meraki → Satori →
Kavanah.

# CROSS-CUTTING

## 8. The opener, the questions you ask them, reading the signals

**8.1 The opener — "tell me about yourself" / the first 90 seconds (Tier B).** Almost every interview opens
here, and it sets the frame. The effective shape is a brief, structured arc: where you are now → relevant
thread of how you got here → why this role/now. Not a life story, not a CV recital. For **career-changers**
this is the single most important answer — it's where you frame the pivot as coherent, not random (Section 11).

**8.2 The questions you ask them (Tier A/B).** Strongly assessed and routinely under-prepared. Good questions
signal genuine interest and judgement: about the team, the work, what success looks like, how they'd describe
the culture honestly. Avoid questions answered by a glance at the website, and avoid leading with
salary/holiday at an early stage. Prepare 3-5; have more than you think you'll need because some get answered
during the conversation.

**8.3 Reading the signals (Tier B).** What interviewers assess sits *behind* the literal question: a "tell me
about a conflict" question is really probing self-awareness and how you treat people; "why us" probes whether
you'll actually take the offer. The advisor can teach the user to hear the underlying question and answer
*that*. Signs an interview is going well (more time than scheduled, future-tense language, introductions to
others) are soft and unreliable — the advisor should not over-read them or let a user spiral on a "bad
feeling," which is notoriously inaccurate.

## 9. Post-interview — follow-up, rejection, feedback, multi-stage, offers

- **Follow-up / thank-you (Tier B).** A short, genuine thank-you note within ~24 hours is low-cost and
  occasionally tips a close decision; reference one specific thing from the conversation. UK norms are lighter
  than US (it's not expected the way it is in the US), so frame it as optional-but-nice, not mandatory.
- **Handling rejection + asking for feedback (Tier A/B).** Rejection is the norm in a 140-applications-per-
  vacancy market (Section 10), not a verdict on worth. It's reasonable to ask, once and graciously, for brief
  feedback; many employers won't give specifics (legal caution), so the advisor should set honest expectations
  and turn any feedback into a concrete next-time action. This is the interview-side companion to "why am I not
  hearing back?".
- **Multi-stage processes (Tier A).** Map the stages up front so the user isn't blindsided (screen → online
  tests → asynchronous video → assessment centre → final panel is a realistic large-scheme sequence). Prep
  ramps with the stage.
- **Salary / offer conversations (Tier B, light scope here).** Flagged, not fully covered: at offer stage the
  user may want help evaluating and negotiating. This overlaps the already-logged **offer-evaluation** feature
  and is largely out of scope for *interview prep* — but the advisor should not refuse it cold; it should give
  honest, basic guidance (research the range, negotiate respectfully, value the whole package) and note when a
  fuller offer-evaluation flow exists. Full grounding deferred (Section 14).

## 10. UK 2026 context

- **The market squeeze (Tier A, with a scope caveat).** ISE Student Recruitment Survey 2025: the average
  graduate vacancy receives **140 applications**; graduate hiring fell **8% year-on-year** while apprentice
  hiring rose 8%. **Important scope:** this figure is for ISE member organisations — large employers and formal
  graduate schemes — and is *not* representative of the whole UK market; someone applying to local SMEs or
  small charities typically faces far lower volumes. The advisor should use 140:1 to normalise rejection for
  scheme applicants (it's the market, not you) **without inflating anxiety for users who aren't applying to big
  schemes** — match the framing to where the user is actually applying. _(ISE: "Student Recruitment Survey
  2025".)_
- **Success Profiles are current (Tier A).** The Civil Service's five-element framework (Behaviours,
  Strengths, Ability, Experience, Technical) remains the live standard on gov.uk — ground public-sector prep in
  it directly. _(GOV.UK: "Success Profiles".)_
- **The AI-in-interviews shift (Tier A for the headline, Tier B for the specifics).** This is the fastest-
  moving piece and the reason for the integrity line. ISE 2025: the most-reported integrity problem was
  **candidates using AI during interviews without disclosure or permission — cited by 61% of employers**; the
  share of employers frequently encountering cheating more than doubled (7%→15%). In response, some major
  employers now **require candidates to acknowledge they won't use AI tools during interviews**, and several
  large firms have **reintroduced in-person interviews** specifically to counter AI-assisted and deepfake
  fraud (Tier B — reported via commercial/industry sources; the ISE 61% figure is the Tier A anchor). On the
  *employer* side, AI screens many asynchronous interviews, with the bias caveats in §1.9. _(ISE 2025; IES on
  AI in interviews; industry reporting — Tier B for named-company policies.)_
  - **What this means for our advice:** the advisor coaches the user to be *genuinely* ready so they don't need
    to cheat, and is explicit that covert AI use is both wrong and increasingly detected. Using AI to *prepare*
    (rehearse, structure, get feedback — exactly what our mock is) is legitimate and encouraged; using it
    *live and concealed* is not. This distinction is a feature of our positioning, not a disclaimer.

## 11. Edge cases

**11.1 No network / first-generation / low social capital (Tier B).** Many of our users have no one to do a
mock with and have never been coached on interviews — which is exactly the gap the advisor-run mock fills (free,
private, unlimited, no social cost). The advisor should assume no prior coaching: explain *why* a question is
asked, not just how to answer it, and never assume "obvious" knowledge (dress, format, what STAR is).

**11.2 Career-changers, returners, and older/experienced candidates (Tier A/B — all-ages scope).**
- **Career-changers:** the make-or-break is the *narrative* — a confident, coherent "why this change" that
  frames past experience as transferable strength, not a false start. Lead the opener with it; address the
  employer's unspoken worry ("is this a passing whim?") directly. _(Prospects: "How to change careers".)_
- **Returners (after caregiving, illness, redundancy):** don't apologise for the gap; acknowledge it briefly
  and pivot to current readiness and transferable skills. Some sectors actively value the maturity/stability
  returners bring. _(Practitioner consensus — Tier B.)_
- **Older candidates:** focus on transferable value and recent upskilling; the advisor must never imply age is
  a barrier, and should counter the user's own ageist self-talk with strategy.

**11.3 International students / visa-holders / English as an additional language (Tier A/B).** Be honest about
sponsorship realities (some employers won't sponsor — that's a filter to know early, consistent with honest
matching). For EAL candidates, the mock is especially valuable for rehearsing fluency and idiom under pressure;
feedback should be on clarity and structure, never on accent. Cultural-norm differences (UK interviews prize
specific personal examples and a degree of modesty-with-evidence) are worth surfacing explicitly.

**11.4 Neurodiversity + reasonable adjustments (Tier A).** Under the **Equality Act 2010**, employers have a
legal duty to make reasonable adjustments to any part of recruitment, including interview format. The advisor
should proactively inform users they can request adjustments and give examples: **questions in advance**,
extra processing time, a phone/video alternative, a quieter environment, clear/literal questions instead of
abstract hypotheticals. This is a rights-and-access point, not a favour — many neurodivergent candidates don't
know they can ask. The advisor must never pathologise; it informs and empowers. _(Equality Act 2010;
autism.org.uk: "Reasonable adjustments"; Employment Autism: "Reasonable adjustments for interviews".)_

**11.5 Regional (Tier B).** Format norms are broadly national, but market density differs (London concentrates
finance/consulting/media; other regions have their own clusters). Minor for interview *format*; relevant for
expectation-setting. Flagged as lower-confidence.

## 12. Logistics (the practical layer)

- **Video setup (Tier B):** test camera, mic, and connection beforehand; look at the lens not the screen;
  neutral, well-lit, quiet space; have water and a notepad; close other apps. For asynchronous, do a practice
  recording and check the retake/timer rules first.
- **In-person:** know the location and travel time; arrive ~10 minutes early; bring copies of your CV.
- **Recovery when things go wrong (Tier B):** connection drops — stay calm, rejoin, a brief apology is fine;
  mind goes blank — it's acceptable to pause, take a breath, or ask to come back to a question; you fluff an
  answer — you can say "actually, let me give a better example." Interviewers expect nerves; composure in
  recovery is itself a positive signal. The advisor should rehearse these recoveries in the mock so they're
  not first encountered live.
- **Dress (Tier B — practitioner / careers-service consensus; UK university careers services publish this
  guidance):** match the sector (formal for law/finance/public sector; smart-casual for much of
  tech/creative/startup); when unsure, slightly smarter is safer. The advisor should give sector-specific
  guidance rather than a blanket rule, and never assume the user knows.

## 13. Build implications — how to ground the advisor (planning tool + mock)

**Hard rules (must encode):**
1. **Establish format first.** The `interview_prep` flow's first move is to determine stage + format (ask, or
   read a pasted invitation), then calibrate everything to it. Never give format-blind "top 10 questions."
2. **Ground named-employer prep in published frameworks.** For Civil Service → Success Profiles behaviours from
   the advert; Amazon → Leadership Principles; EY/Big Four → strengths. Use the employer's own public material;
   for everything else, invite the user to paste what they've found (the `foundContext` paste pattern). Never
   invent a specific employer's process.
3. **Teach STAR/STARR for competency; teach self-knowledge (not scripts) for strengths.** Different machinery
   for different formats — don't STAR a strengths interview.
4. **The mock runs the exposure-plus-feedback loop:** one question at a time, real follow-ups, no mid-answer
   coaching, then behaviourally-specific feedback with one concrete fix and an optional re-run. **Never a
   score, never empty praise** (no-gamification + honesty).
5. **Integrity line, explicitly:** coach genuine readiness; encourage AI for *preparation*; state plainly that
   covert live AI use is wrong and increasingly detected. Never help fabricate experience or game an assessment.
6. **Reasonable adjustments are surfaced proactively** for any user who signals a disability or neurodivergence
   (and offered generally), framed as a right under the Equality Act 2010.
7. **Anxiety: validate briefly, then one concrete action.** Reframe arousal, offer a grounding/breathing
   technique, redirect outward. Never minimise, never over-narrate caveats.

**Strong defaults:**
- Build the 5-8 STARR story bank with the user from their real profile (the advisor already holds it).
- Always prepare "questions to ask them" and a structured opener.
- Set honest market expectations so rejection isn't read as personal failure (use 140:1 for big-scheme
  applicants; scale the framing down for users applying to SMEs/small employers — §10 caveat); turn any
  feedback into a next-time action.
- Map multi-stage processes up front; ramp prep per stage.
- Calibrate dress/logistics/format by sector; never assume the user knows the unwritten norms.

**Calibrations (per situation):**
- **Career-changer/returner/older:** lead on the narrative and transferable value; address the unspoken worry.
- **No-network/first-gen:** explain the *why* behind questions; assume no prior coaching.
- **International/EAL/visa:** honest sponsorship reality; mock for fluency; feedback on clarity not accent.
- **Sector:** use the §3 matrix to set the dominant format and emphasis.

**Reuse:** the company-specific intelligence capability (FEATURE-ROADMAP, 2026-06-27) lands first here and is
reused across CV tailoring, outreach, and "why am I not hearing back?".

## 14. Known gaps / lower-confidence areas

Named explicitly so omissions are visible, not silent:

1. **No UK primary RCT on mock-interview efficacy in our exact cohort.** The anxiety-down / performance-up
   direction is supported by a peer-reviewed student-perception study (Wilkie & Rosendale 2024) and the
   broader practice-and-feedback and arousal-reappraisal literature (Brooks 2014), but not by a UK-population
   randomised trial in our cohort. Safe to build on the *direction*; don't quote effect sizes to users.
2. **Named-employer AI-interview policies are Tier B.** The ISE 61% figure (candidates using AI undisclosed) is
   Tier A; specific "Company X now bans/reverts" claims come from industry reporting and shift fast — verify
   before stating a named company's current policy to a user. Re-ground at the review date.
3. **Asynchronous-AI scoring internals are opaque.** How any given employer's AI scores a video is proprietary
   and contested; we can prepare candidates to perform well to camera but cannot reverse-engineer a specific
   tool. Stated honestly rather than faked.
4. **Salary/offer negotiation is only lightly covered here** — deliberately, as it belongs to the
   offer-evaluation feature. Needs its own grounding pass (UK negotiation norms, sector ranges) before that
   feature is built.
5. **Sector matrix weightings are Tier B in several rows.** The dominant format per sector is sound; exact
   emphasis varies by individual employer and should be flexed, not asserted.
6. **Regional differences (§11.5) are lightly evidenced.** Format is broadly national; market density differs.
7. **Apprenticeship / non-graduate-scheme interview norms** are less documented than graduate-scheme processes;
   covered via the general competency/values guidance but with thinner sector-specific sourcing.
8. **Academic/research interview specifics** (seminar/job-talk conventions) are summarised at low depth — a
   minority of our users; expand if demand appears.
9. **Internal promotion / step-up interviews** (an internal panel for a role above your current one) differ
   from external hiring — panel composition, assumed existing knowledge, internal politics — and are not
   separately covered here. Relevant to returners/career-changers moving up internally; a small cohort. Expand
   if demand appears.
